## Read this first: corrections and surprises

1. **Ireland's chapter has a different published title.** The library catalog record for Brenda Laurel (ed.), *Design Research: Methods and Perspectives* (MIT Press, 2003) lists Christopher Ireland's chapter as "Qualitative Methods: From Boring to Brilliant", after her section introduction "The Changing Role of Research". No listing uses the course title "From giving voice to interpreting meaning". Confidence: high for the title.
2. **The strategy sequence taught in class matches Shedroff, but the brief's list mixes in extra items.** Your own Week 4 class notes list the sequence as Customer, Segment, Competitor or Alternative, Position, then Opportunity, Offering, Capability, Partner, with the rule "THEN, AND ONLY THEN, What could the offering be?" Shedroff's 2021 deck lists the strategy steps as environmental analysis, customer analysis, customer segmentation, competitive analysis, positioning, infrastructure development, and so on. In the book, trends and stakeholders are a separate chapter (10, "Trends & Stakeholders", in the part "The World Around You"). "Decision drivers" and "threats" are ideas inside the steps, not steps. Confidence: high.
3. **Fogg renamed "triggers" to "prompts" in late 2017, and the ability factors changed from six to five.** The 2009 paper lists six simplicity factors (Time, Money, Physical Effort, Brain Cycles, Social Deviance, Non-Routine). Fogg's current "Ability Chain" figure shows five: time, money, physical effort, mental effort, routine. Social deviance is gone. The curve on the chart is now labeled "Action Line". Confidence: high (figures viewed on behaviormodel.org).
4. **"Triggers" means two different things in this course.** Fogg's trigger (now prompt) is the cue that tells a person to act now. Shedroff's "triggers" dimension is the set of stimuli that make up an experience: sight, sound, touch, smell, taste, concepts, symbols, name, and price. Confidence: high.
5. **Guest, Bunce, and Johnson did not prove that 12 interviews is always enough.** They showed it for 60 structured interviews with a homogeneous group, and they warn that 12 "will likely not be enough" for heterogeneous groups, poor data, or vague topics. Confidence: high (full paper read).
6. **The Jones and Samalionis case is 1st Source Bank, not Bank of America.** IDEO's "Keep the Change" project does not appear in the article. Confidence: high (full article read).
7. **The waveline is not a journey map, and Shedroff says so on a slide.** His slide "JOURNEY MAP vs WAVELINE" says a journey map maps touchpoints and functions, while a waveline maps experiences ("change in people"), emotions, values, meaning, and value, and places opportunities in context and time. His experience template defines it in one line: "Charting intensity over time is called a Waveline." Confidence: high.
8. **Shedroff's experience dimensions exist in two name sets.** Older decks say Significance, Breadth, Intensity, Duration, Triggers, Interaction. His current template and website say Value, Breadth, Intensity, Duration, Triggers, Interaction (the website says "Interactivity"). He calls Value "the most important dimension". Confidence: high.
9. **Shedroff says you should never write another SWOT, and he says why.** His book introduction states that "a SWOT isn't even necessary as it emerges automatically from the other steps in the process. You should never draft another SWOT in your career!" Confidence: high.
10. **The term "affordance" dates from 1966, not the late 1970s.** James J. Gibson coined it in his 1966 book and gave his best-known definition in 1979. Donald Norman brought it into design in 1988 and added "signifiers" in 2013. Confidence: high (Wikipedia, checked).
11. **The Shedroff videos date from 2021 and 2024.** YouTube shows Design Thinking, Great Design, Typography, 5 Kinds of Value, Stakeholder Analysis, and Fixing the SWOT published in August and September 2021; Experience Dimensions, Continuous Strategy, and The Waveline published on 28 September 2024 (the course lists the last three as 2025). Confidence: high.
12. **Context worth knowing.** Columbia's School of Professional Studies lists Nathan Shedroff as a Lecturer in its M.S. in Technology Management, teaching "TMGT PS5112: Design-Based Innovation Programs". That explains why so many readings are his tools. I could not confirm whether he teaches your section.

## Week 1: Design thinking, great design, experience dimensions, critique

### 1.1 Auernhammer and Roth (2021): where Stanford's design thinking came from

**Core idea.**
- Design thinking at Stanford grew out of John Arnold's "Creative Engineering" teaching from 1957, which drew on humanistic psychology (Maslow, Rogers) and creativity research (Guilford). Humanistic psychology is the school that centers human needs, values, and growth.
- The authors screened 2,617 Stanford course descriptions (1,860 retained) and 78 documents from 1957 to 2005, and found eight "design qualities" that stayed constant.
- Their central claim: design thinking is a culture and a set of human capabilities. They "question the notion that DT is a construct that can be 'used' or 'implemented' through a step-by-step process and tools."

**The five periods.**

| Period | Years | What happened | Key people |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Early influences | Before 1957 | Psychology of creativity, needs, and productive thinking | Guilford, Maslow, Rogers, Wertheimer, Dreyfuss |
| 2. The beginning | 1957 to 1964 | Arnold's Creative Engineering; 1959 summer program; Joint Program in Product Design (official start 1963) | Arnold, McKim, Kahn, Maslow, Hartman |
| 3. Growth and entrepreneurship | 1965 to 1985 | McKim's visual thinking and need-finding (course ME116b, 1967); Adams's "Conceptual Blockbusting"; the Design Loft; new companies such as Powell-Peralta, Concept2, Apple Computer, and Hovey-Kelley Design (later IDEO); Center for Design Research (1984) | McKim, Adams, Roth, Leifer |
| 4. Design thinking | 1986 to 2005 | Faste's "ambidextrous thinking"; design as a social process; IDEO co-founded by Moggridge, Kelley, and Nuttall two years after 1989; Leifer's 1999 course with "Design Thinking" in its title; ABC Nightline's "The Deep Dive" (1999); the d.school founded in 2005 with Hasso Plattner's funding | Faste, Leifer, Kelley, Moggridge |
| 5. Design thinking in innovation management | After 2005 | Business literature adopts the term (Brown 2008, Martin 2009) | Brown, Martin |

**The eight design qualities (the paper's Figure 3).**

| Quality | What it contains |
|---|---|
| Thinking Modes | Ambidextrous thinking: analysis and seeing, synthesis and imagining, evaluation and drawing |
| Attitudes and Values | A questioning attitude; human values |
| Attributes | Need and problem sensitivity, fluency, and flexibility; motivation, confidence, and drive |
| Abilities | Switching between thinking languages; visualizing and communicating; interdisciplinary teamwork |
| Blocks | Perceptual; expressive and intellectual; emotional; cultural and environmental |
| Activities and Practices | Need-finding, problem definition, visualization, prototyping, refinement, testing |
| Techniques | Storytelling, scenarios, synectics (structured analogy), experience prototypes, role-playing, brainstorming |
| Environment | Physical space (Design Loft, machine shop); help and support; psychological safety and freedom |

**Quotes worth using.**
- Robert McKim (1959): "Design is, after all, a response to human needs."
- Arnold on his four creative attitudes (questioning, observing, associating, predicting): "I don't actually like to think of them as steps of a process that are followed in a certain definite sequence."
- Practitioner point: "Design thinking as a step-by-step process with tools prevents fluency in thinking and flexibility in approach."
- Without the design qualities, design thinking "is performed in a linear cookbook pattern fashion and with little regard to aesthetics and human value."

**Tool or artifact.** A capability checklist of eight design qualities. It is not a process diagram.

**Applied to PawPop.**
- Restarting from scratch is a chance to do need-finding before naming a solution: a dog run in the morning, a lobby when a walker collects a dog, the adoption floor at Animal Care Centers of NYC (ACC).
- Prototype tangibly and early, for example a paper "who may collect this dog" card at a front desk.
- Name the team's blocks. The earlier PawPal deck shows one: the concept stayed an app from the first slide to the last ("A social app" became "A practical local tool" and "a local app").

**Critical take.**
- Strong: a large archive; a clear structure for a fuzzy term; an explanation of why process theater fails.
- Weak: one institution, told by insiders (Bernard Roth co-founded the d.school and is its Academic Director). It is a history, not a test of whether design thinking improves outcomes. It analyzes only Stanford's lineage. It gives little operational guidance for a team on a deadline.

**Source.** Auernhammer, J., and Roth, B. (2021). *Journal of Product Innovation Management* 38(6), 623-644. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpim.12594 (open access). Read in full. Confidence: high.

### 1.4 Staats: How to give powerful (and effective) design critique

**Core idea.** A critique improves a design against its goals. It works best when the critic asks first, keeps the end user central, and gives specific, evidence-based direction instead of taste.

**The five rules (the article's headings).**
1. "#1: Lead with questions."
2. "#2: Talk about the positives." The article recommends the "sandwich": praise, then constructive feedback, then praise.
3. "#3: Think about the user's perspective."
4. "#4: Offer directional suggestions."
5. "#5: Provide objective, data-backed feedback."

Pro tip from the article: "Approach corrective design feedback in the passive voice. So, instead of saying Why did you choose to add this image?, say Why was this image chosen for this [website area]?" After the session: written notes of agreed changes, a timeline, and a follow-up.

**Related, verified.** Christina Wodtke's critique format for her CCA class (section 5.6) is close to rule 1: "Questions first. Then 3 keeps and changes. Finally ideas to explore."

**Tool or artifact.** A critique protocol.

**Applied to PawPop.** Start every team critique with "Which stakeholder does this serve, and which interview finding supports it?" End with written changes and an owner for each.

**Critical take.**
- Strong: short, teachable, user-centered.
- Weak: an agency blog post, not research. The sandwich can bury the message and teach people to discount praise (my assessment). "Data-backed" is hard at the idea stage. Nothing on receiving critique.

**Source.** Staats, R., "How to give powerful (and effective) design critique", Secret Stache, https://www.secretstache.com/blog/design-critique/. Raw text checked. Confidence: high.

## Week 2: Interviews, qualitative methods, value, stakeholders, customers

### 2.1 Guest, Bunce, and Johnson (2006): How many interviews are enough?

**Core idea.**
- The authors coded 60 interviews in ten rounds of six and tracked when new codes (labels for themes) stopped appearing.
- Saturation is "the point in data collection and analysis when new information produces little or no change to the codebook" (the team's list of theme labels and definitions).
- Saturation arrived by about twelve interviews; the basic elements of the four high-level themes ("metathemes") were present after six.

**The study.** 30 women in greater Accra (Ghana) and 30 in Ibadan (Nigeria), all at high risk for HIV, interviewed in 2003 about social desirability bias in self-reported sexual behavior. Same questions in the same order for everyone.

**The numbers.**

| Measure | After 6 | After 12 | Final |
|---|---|---|---|
| Codes in the Ghana data (109 after 30 interviews) | 80 (73%) | 100 (92%) | 109 |
| Codes across both countries (114 after 60) | 80 | 100 (88%) | 114 (Nigeria added 5) |
| High-frequency codes (36 at the end) | 34 (94%) | 35 (97%) | 36 |
| Codebook definition changes (36 total) | 4 (11%) | 21 cumulative (58%) | 36 |

**Their recommendations and limits (quoted).**
- "For most research enterprises, however, in which the aim is to understand common perceptions and experiences among a group of relatively homogeneous individuals, twelve interviews should suffice."
- Twelve "will likely not be enough if a selected group is relatively heterogeneous, the data quality is poor, and the domain of inquiry is diffuse and/or vague."
- Not applicable to "unstructured and highly exploratory interview techniques."
- To compare groups, one "might purposively select twelve participants per group of interest."
- For high-level themes only, "a sample of six interviews may have been sufficient."
- They "caution against assuming that six to twelve interviews will always be enough" or using the result to justify "quick and dirty" research.
- Correlation between variables needs far more (they cite Graves: 60 to 120).
- They cite Nielsen and Landauer (1993): six evaluators found about 80% of major usability problems, leveling off near 90% at about twelve.

**Tool or artifact.** A sample-size plan per segment, plus a codebook in which you count new codes per batch.

**Applied to PawPop.**
- NYC dog life is heterogeneous: borough, income, building type, dog size, and role. Plan about 12 interviews per homogeneous segment, not 12 in total.
- Your earlier deck's next step was "Talk to dog owners in one neighbourhood". That is a sensible scope: one neighborhood and one role make a more homogeneous group.
- Keep a fixed core of questions; count new themes after each batch of four to six; stop when a batch adds almost nothing.

**Critical take.**
- Strong: transparent, empirical, with candid caveats.
- Weak: one homogeneous population, one topic, one team's codebook. Saturation depends on coding granularity (the authors' "lumper-splitter problem"). Later studies found higher numbers for themes across sites (Hagaman and Wutich 2017: 20 to 40 across four sites) and for "meaning saturation" (Hennink, Kaiser, and Marconi 2017: 16 to 24); these two come from search summaries, moderate confidence.

**Source.** Guest, G., Bunce, A., and Johnson, L. (2006). *Field Methods* 18(1), 59-82. https://doi.org/10.1177/1525822X05279903 (copy: https://qualquant.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/guest-2006-how-many-interviews.FM_.pdf). Read in full. Confidence: high.

### 2.3 Shedroff: The 5 kinds of value (special depth)

**Core idea.**
- Video description (7 Sep 2021, 25.9 min): the episode "describes the 5 kinds of value and how traditional business tools and processes focus on only the 2 simplest (price and features) and not the 3 most important (emotional, identity, and meaningful)."
- His 2021 deck: "Value comes from relationships which occur in experiences." "Those companies (and people) who focus on premium value create more of it, more often."
- His website: "Recognizing that there are more kinds of value than just money is the first step in building more and better value."

**The five kinds, with his own definitions and the question each answers (questions from his 2021 deck).**

| Kind | His question | His definition | Measurable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional | "Does this do what I need...?" | "what products and services do for people in a, yes, functional sense: features and performance" | Quantitative |
| Financial | "...at a price I feel is worth it?" | "measured in money. It's the easiest to understand because it's the easiest to measure" | Quantitative |
| Emotional | "How does this make me feel?" | "the emotional reaction that someone feels when they see, buy, use, or have something" (LinkedIn version of his article) | Qualitative |
| Identity | "Is this me?" | "The things we associate with ourselves, surround ourselves with, buy, and use help define us." When they do not fit our sense of identity, "we feel 'off.'" | Qualitative |
| Meaningful | "Does this fit into my worldview?" | We "feel more 'whole' when certain criteria are met in our lives", captured by the 15 core meanings | Qualitative |

- "These last three kinds of value are qualitative, making them more difficult to measure than the first two." His slides label the three "(Premium)".
- His template notes that the values "closer to the center are both more stable and more 'valuable'" (meaning sits at the center, function at the edge).
- His Instagram example (slides): total value $1.1B, of which $86M was book value and $1.01B was "premium" or "good will" value.

**The 15 core meanings** (from *Making Meaning*, Diller, Shedroff, and Rhea, 2005; definitions at makingmeaning.org per his slides): Accomplishment, Beauty, Creation, Community, Duty, Enlightenment, Freedom, Harmony, Justice, Oneness, Redemption, Security, Truth, Validation, Wonder. His slide: "There are 15 core meanings that define how people frame the world around them. People can prioritize the same core meanings yet express them differently."

How to use them (nathan.com): find customers' top 3 to 5 meanings and compare with those of your organization, employees, and competitors; an overlap of 2 to 3 is strong, 0 to 1 signals difficulty; "base your customer segments on their core meaning priorities."

**Applied to PawPop.**
- Your earlier deck named "Two kinds of need: Functional (find information and plan activities) and Emotional (community and confidence)." That covers two of the five. Missing: financial (what owners pay now, for example walkers or daycare), identity ("Is this me?": a good owner, a rescue person), and meaningful (which core meanings dog owners hold).
- Meanings to test in interviews (hypotheses): Duty, Community (the team's file cites a study in which dog owners were about 5 times as likely as other pet owners to get to know neighbors), Security, Validation, Wonder.

**Critical take.**
- Strong: breaks price-and-features thinking; one vocabulary across research, strategy, and experience.
- Weak: the qualitative kinds are hard to measure and easy to assert; emotional, identity, and meaning overlap; the Instagram example treats all goodwill as "premium value", which is an interpretation of an accounting residual, not a measurement (my assessment). Designing for identity and meaning without delivering function can become manipulation.

**Source.** https://nathan.com/5-kinds-of-value/ ; https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/financial-value-isnt-most-important-business-nathan-shedroff (16 Sep 2019) ; https://nathan.com/tool-meaning-model-strategy/ ; 2021 deck (link in 1.3) ; video https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RZ9vVq2YqDA. Raw text and slides checked. Confidence: high.

### 2.5 *A Whole New Strategy*, Chapter 1: "Your Customers"

**What is verified.**

### 3.1 Beaujean, Davidson, and Madge (2006): The "moment of truth" in customer service (special depth)

**Core idea (definition verified through a verbatim quote).** A 2007 commentary quotes the article: "That spark and the emotionally driven behavior that creates it explain how great customer service companies earn trust and loyalty during 'moments of truth': those few interactions (for instance, a lost credit card, a canceled flight, a damaged piece of clothing, or investment advice) when customers invest a high amount of emotional energy in the outcome. Superb handling of these moments requires an instinctive frontline response that puts the customer's emotional needs ahead of the company's and the employee's agendas." A 2014 book chapter cites the same phrase ("invest a high amount of emotional energy in the outcome") to McKinsey Quarterly 2006, no. 1, p. 62.

**What the article recommends (as summarized by the same commentary, which notes the article draws on Daniel Goleman's emotional intelligence).**
1. Get meaning into people's work, presented simply; employees will not react spontaneously "if they feel the weight of a lengthy and detailed rule book."
2. Use learning through experience: "People learn emotionally intelligent behavior when they become aware of their own inhibiting mind-sets."
3. Align structures, systems, and processes, and simplify frontline processes.
4. Enlist frontline leaders and mentors as role models: "children watch their parents; employees watch their leaders."

**Not confirmed.** Secondary summaries attribute to the article a bank study in Belgium, Germany, and Italy in which more than 85 percent of customers with a positive moment of truth increased their business, more than 70 percent of those with a negative one reduced it, and share of wallet differed by about 20 percent. McKinsey's site refused every automated route I tried (the fetch tool timed out three times, curl timed out or was reset, Node received HTTP 403, and the Wayback Machine and archive.today refused). Open the article in a browser before putting these numbers on a slide.

**Where the term comes from (verified).** A 2014 book chapter, "Service and Moments of Truth" (in *Designing Service Excellence*), explains that Richard Normann took the term from bullfighting ("el momento de verdad"), and that Jan Carlzon, who led SAS airline from 1981, estimated "50 million moments of truth" a year, each decided in about fifteen seconds, and wrote *Moments of Truth* (1987). Wikipedia confirms that Procter & Gamble's A. G. Lafley coined the "first moment of truth" in 2005 and Google coined the "zero moment of truth" (online research before purchase) in 2011. Those are different ideas from McKinsey's emotional service moments.

**Tool or artifact.** A short list of moments of truth per segment, with what good handling looks like, plus preparation of the people who handle them.

**How to find them (my synthesis).** List every interaction; rate each on emotional stakes and frequency; select the few with the highest stakes; observe how they are handled; design, train, and measure.

**Applied to PawPop** (facts from the team's file 05).
- **The vet's money moment.** In a 2025 Gallup study, 52% of owners skipped or declined recommended care, 71% of them because of cost; 73% of those said the vet offered no cheaper option, while 81% of vets say they always or often recommend an alternative.

**Critical take.**

- Weak: bank data on stated behavior; a practitioner article; it can push the burden onto low-paid frontline staff instead of fixing the system around them.

### 3.2 Jones and Samalionis (2008): From small ideas to radical service innovation

**Core idea.**
- Radical innovation means "new services that dramatically change the marketplace", yet service firms usually deliver incremental tweaks. A McKinsey survey from October 2007 found more than 70 percent of corporate leaders named innovation among their top three priorities.
- Barriers: silos built for efficiency, competing agendas, no consistent champion, ill-defined success measures, scale, the cost of change.
- Mistakes: calling incremental changes radical; killing ideas too soon; choosing ideas by operational fit instead of customer need; weak champions; no prototyping; no overarching goal.
- IDEO's answer: stay expansive early, then apply "consumer desirability, technical feasibility, and business viability".

**The five stages.**

| Stage | The article's advice |
|---|---|
| 1. Develop insight about the market | "Develop insights about customers, the business, and technology in parallel." "Develop frameworks that clearly describe the 'pain point' and the opportunity space." Excitement over customer insights alone leads to early brainstorming that "tends to result in incremental service improvements." |
| 2. Create radical value propositions | "Involve front-line personnel and executives simultaneously." "Prototype extreme service propositions early to stretch the organizational mindset." Use experience prototypes that look and behave like the service without being built like it. "Conduct formative research to tease out desirability, viability, and feasibility." |
| 3. Explore creative service models | "Challenge the existing operational realities." "Champion customer desirability as viability and feasibility are modeled." |
| 4. Bend the rules of delivery | "Get permission to fail." "Teams that are afraid to fail make radical innovation, by definition, impossible." "Design new metrics for measuring success" (a health-insurance call center lengthened calls on purpose and moved to health-outcome metrics). |
| 5. Iteratively pilot and refine the new service | "Don't wait for the service to be perfect; get comfortable with beta." "Use the time during piloting to prepare for large-scale rollout." |

**The diagram.** The x-axis runs from "focus on customer desirability" to "focus on business viability and technical feasibility"; the y-axis from "looking at existing realities for inspiration" to "letting go of the existing realities to envision an alternative world."

**The case: 1st Source Bank, South Bend, Indiana (2004).** Planning to grow branches by 50 percent, the bank rethought the branch with IDEO; a full-scale foam-core mock-up tested side-by-side banking; a pilot ran in part of one branch with adapted software. Converted branches showed new accounts, deposit growth, and loan sales "all more than 30 percent higher than for similar branches lacking the new service model."

**Applied to PawPop.** Your earlier deck already proposed "Start in one neighbourhood", which is stage 5 thinking. Add stage 2: involve frontline people (walkers, doormen, cafe staff) early, and prototype one extreme proposition, for example "the building, not the app, is the unit of dog care". Add stage 4: choose metrics such as incidents avoided rather than downloads.

**Critical take.** Consultants describing their own process; the 30 percent result has no stated method or time frame; "radical" is never defined measurably.

**Source.** Jones, M., and Samalionis, F. (2008). *Design Management Review* 19(1), 20-27. https://new-ideo-com.s3.amazonaws.com/assets/files/pdfs/news/08191JON20.pdf. Read in full. Confidence: high.

### 3.3 Fogg Behavior Model (special depth)

**Core idea.**
- 2009 abstract: a person must "(1) be sufficiently motivated, (2) have the ability to perform the behavior, and (3) be triggered to perform the behavior. These three factors must occur at the same moment, else the behavior will not happen."
- Current site: "Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt come together at the same time." "Without a Prompt, the target behavior will not happen." Fogg: "I once called this element the 'Trigger.' I changed this term in late 2017. Now I use 'Prompt.'"

**The chart (current figure, "Fogg Behavior Model", © 2007 BJ Fogg).** Vertical axis: Motivation, low to high. Horizontal axis: Ability, "Hard to Do" to "Easy to Do". A curve labeled "Action Line" separates "Prompts succeed here" (above) from "Prompts fail here" (below). Caption: "B=MAP at the same moment". The 2009 paper calls the curve the "behavior activation threshold" and notes "there are no units on this axis, as the framework is conceptual." Fogg's motivation page: motivation and ability have a "compensatory relationship", and "That's what the curved line on my Behavior Model represents."

**Core motivators.** Current site: "three Core Motivators: Sensation, Anticipation, and Belonging. Each of these has two sides: pleasure/pain, hope/fear, acceptance/rejection." The 2009 paper names them Pleasure/Pain, Hope/Fear, Social Acceptance/Rejection, and calls hope "probably the most ethical and empowering motivator."

**Ability.**

| 2009 paper (six simplicity factors) | Current "Ability Chain" figure (five links) |
|---|---|
| Time | time |
| Money | money |
| Physical Effort | physical effort |
| Brain Cycles | mental effort |
| Social Deviance | (dropped) |
| Non-Routine | routine |

- 2009: "Simplicity is a function of a person's scarcest resource at the moment a behavior is triggered." Current: "Key insight: Simplicity is a function of your scarcest resource at that moment." "Your weakest link determines what makes a behavior hard to do."
- Three paths to more ability (current site): train people ("That's the hard path"), give a tool or resource, or scale the behavior back ("the approach I advocate in the Tiny Habits® method").
- 2009: "persuasive design succeeds faster when we focus on making the behavior simpler instead of trying to pile on motivation."

**Prompt types (Fogg's figure, © 2008).**

| Type | When | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Facilitator | High motivation, low ability | Makes the behavior easier |
| Spark | High ability, low motivation | Carries motivation with the cue |
| Signal | High ability, high motivation | Simply reminds |

2009: a working trigger is noticed, associated with the behavior, and arrives when the person is motivated and able (the Greek *kairos*, the opportune moment). "As recipients, we'll be most tolerant of triggers when they are signals or facilitators. Sparks may annoy us."

**Tool or artifact.** A diagnosis of one specific target behavior: prompt present? weakest ability link at that moment? missing motivator? Fix the cheapest factor first, usually ability or the prompt.

**Applied to PawPop** (facts from the team's file 05; diagnoses are hypotheses).
- **Owner bags and bins waste.** Complaints rise while summonses are near zero. Motivation (Belonging) mostly exists; ability fails when bags run out or bins are far (physical effort, routine). Fix ability.
- **Owner answers the Health Department after a bite.** About half do. A facilitator fits: one check-in link that says what happens next.
- **Owner acts on a stranger's tip or meets a stranger's dog** (your earlier deck's "Enough trust to act?"). Motivation exists (Belonging, Anticipation); ability is limited by mental effort (is this person safe?) and routine. A facilitator such as first meetups at public dog-friendly places (which your deck proposed) lowers the effort.

**Critical take.**
- Strong: simple, shared vocabulary; moves design from exhortation to simplification and timing.
- Weak: the 2009 paper presents no experiments; with no units the model can explain almost anything after the fact; it can power manipulative designs.

**Source.** Fogg, B. J. (2009), *Persuasive '09*, https://doi.org/10.1145/1541948.1541999 (read in full from `C:\Users\Admin\Downloads\Columbia Lectures\User Centric Design and Innovation\1541948.1541999 - A behaviour model for persuasive design.pdf`). https://behaviormodel.org/ , /prompts , /motivation , /ability (text and figures checked). Confidence: high.

### 3.5 Stakeholder value map (special depth): what it shows and how to draw it

**What it shows.**
- Everyone with a stake in an offering, inside and outside the organization, and the value flowing between them. "Stakeholders are different than shareholders."
- Map all kinds of value, because that reveals what others miss, for example "where a lot of value is flowing into but not out of a stakeholder."
- Shedroff is candid that "these tools are sorely underdeveloped." His templates: Simple External, Combined Internal/External, Personal/User.

**His template (2021 deck).** "Your Organization" in the center. An inner ring of internal functions: leadership (management), board of directors, strategy, research and development, sales (business development), legal, manufacturing, operations, customer service, marketing, product development, accounting, human resources, IT/technology. An outer ring of external stakeholders: customers (end users), wholesalers, retailers, distributors, suppliers and manufacturers, partners, competitors, shareholders, investors, institutional investors, banks, insurers and reinsurers, industry trade associations, labor unions, NGOs, media, community (geographic), local government, regional government, courts, and The Environment (Nature). His advice: "Expand your research to other stakeholders, when you can."

**How to draw it (steps 1 to 3 follow his template; steps 4 to 7 are my synthesis of his page).**
1. Put the focal party in the center: the organization, or for the Personal/User template, one person (for PawPop, an NYC dog owner).
2. Fill the inner ring with internal roles and the outer ring with external stakeholders. For PawPop, add the dog.
3. Keep the list broad at first; drop parties only after asking whether they matter.
4. Draw one arrow per exchange and direction, labeled with what flows ("pay per walk", "keys", "peace of mind", "behavior notes").
5. Code each arrow by the five kinds of value, and mark non-value flows such as information, access, and trust.
6. Read it for imbalances, value pooling in one party, missing flows, and parties with no voice.
7. Validate with interviews, date it, and redraw after changes.

**PawPop worked example (facts from the team's file 05).**

| Stakeholder | Gives the owner or dog | Gets back | Imbalance or gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dog walker | Exercise, eyes on the dog (functional); peace of mind (emotional) | Pay minus a 20% (Rover) to about 40% (Wag) cut | Receives no behavior or health history |
| Doorman or super | Access, key custody | Tips, goodwill | Legally counts as the landlord's knowledge, yet has no formal role or information |
| Vet | Health; relief and grief support | Money; also blame and abuse | The cost conversation fails when money enters |
| Neighbor without a dog | Tolerance, shared space | Little | Bears bites, waste, noise |
| ACC shelter | A dog; meaning ("I rescued") | Adoptions, fosters | Behavior notes rarely travel with the dog |
| Cafe or restaurant | Welcome, community | Foot traffic | Complaints and inspection risk |

Your earlier deck's "Empty at launch" problem is a stakeholder problem: a map shows who must give value before anyone gets it, and which partners (cafes, vets, groomers, shelters, all listed in your deck) could supply it.

**Critical take.** By Shedroff's own account the tools are underdeveloped; maps get crowded; arrows carry no quantities; the dog cannot be interviewed.

**Source.** https://nathan.com/tool-stakeholder-analysis/ ; 2021 deck, slides 61, 82, 104. Raw text checked. Confidence: high.

### 4.2 Shedroff: Fixing the SWOT analysis (special depth)

**What a classic SWOT is.** A 2-by-2 grid of Strengths and Weaknesses (internal) and Opportunities and Threats (external), usually brainstormed in one session.

**His worked example (introduction).** A fictional startup splits into two pairs; one fills a downloaded SWOT ("Strengths: We're all from Ivy League schools ..."), the other a positioning statement ("As opposed to our competitor, Tired Corp ..."). His verdict: "What's wrong with this approach? Nearly everything. First, none of the identified factors in either tool correspond to the items identified in the other." "None of these entries are validated in any way." "Do customers care that all four of these founders graduated from Ivy League schools?"

**Problems and fixes.**

| Problem | His words (verified) | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Unvalidated opinions | "none of it is validated. It was literally just stuff that came off the top of your head, sitting in a room with a whiteboard." (podcast) | Base entries on customer research and decision drivers |
| Market and operations mixed | "operational issues almost always crowd out the market issues" (podcast); templates "mix internal & external factors" (2021 deck) | Separate them; market first |
| Built in parallel with the positioning statement | "These processes were done in parallel, with no corresponding sequence" (introduction) | Run steps in order |
| Strengths are things the team is proud of | The Ivy League example; "What really drives customer decisions? Hint: It's not identified in any of these!" (2021 deck) | A strength counts only if it scores on a decision driver |
| The SWOT treated as the strategy | "a SWOT isn't even necessary as it emerges automatically from the other steps in the process. You should never draft another SWOT in your career!" | The SWOT becomes a by-product |
| A one-time exercise | Continuous strategy page | Keep it live |

**Strengths and weaknesses are relative to competitors.** A 2014 UXmatters review of his 2013 UX STRAT keynote quotes him defining a strength as where "you're strong and others are weak" and a weakness as where "you're weak and others are strong", and gives his competitive analysis rule: "Discover your biggest strengths versus your competitors' biggest weaknesses and your biggest weaknesses versus your competitors' biggest strengths." On the podcast: "It's not that the hammer's bad; it is that we're taught to use it poorly."

They read as a brainstormed list: "Convenience: Growing demand ..." has no source, and "Competition" lists competitors without comparing them on any driver. **Critical take.** The 14.3-minute video (4 Sep 2021) could not be transcribed, so its exact steps are not quoted here. "Never draft another SWOT" overstates the case; a SWOT that summarizes validated findings still communicates well, as his hammer remark concedes.

### 5.2 Interaction Design Foundation (2023): Modes and modal interfaces (not located)

Caps Lock is the classic example. A mode error happens when the active mode is not clear; NN/g describes a plane crash in which the same control meant a descent angle in one mode and a descent rate in another. Guidance: show the active mode clearly, avoid modes in safety-critical settings, and consider spring-loaded modes ("quasimodes", from Jef Raskin's *The Humane Interface*) that last only while a key is held.

**Applied to PawPop.** A walking tool has modes ("walk in progress", "idle"). If location tracking silently stays off, the walker believes the walk is recorded when it is not. Show the mode on the lock screen; avoid modal pop-ups when one hand holds a leash.

**Source.** Laubheimer, P. (2019), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/modes/. Confidence: high for NN/g; the IxDF piece unverified.

### 5.4 Shedroff: The waveline (special depth)

**Core idea.**
- Definition (his experience template): "Charting intensity over time is called a Waveline."
- Origin (video description, 28 Sep 2024, 28 min): "The Waveline is an established tool from the world of music composition that is particularly well-suited for designing better experiences and customer relationships."
- Purpose (nathan.com): "expressly designed to bridge the worlds, cultures, and understandings between business, engineering, and design peers"; focused on "customer experiences and the customer narrative that gets created in emotional and meaningful terms"; it "charts and highlights where expectations fall the shortest from the reality (setting the highest-impact priorities)."
- It sits inside his chain "value sits within a relationship; relationships sit within an experience" (2021 deck), and it is a core tool of *Blind Spot* (Diller, Shedroff, and Sauber, Rosenfeld Media, 2016).

**Journey map versus waveline (his slide).** Journey map: "Maps touchpoints", "Maps functions", "Design 4 touchpoints". Waveline: "Design 4 relationships", "Maps experiences ('change in people')", "Maps emotions, values & meaning", "Maps value (and, opportunities)", "Can map triggers", "Places opportunities in context & time". (Which bullets sit in which column is my reading of the slide's text order.)

**Axes and stages.**
- Vertical axis: INTENSITY. Horizontal axis: TIME. Both labels appear on his PC example and his template; the template marks the vertical direction from "UNDESIRABLE" to "DESIRABLE" and shows an "IDEAL" and a "CURRENT" line.
- Template phases along time: Initiation, Immersion, Conclusion, Continuation.
- Examples use their own stages: the PC waveline (© Scansion 2014) uses Exploration, Purchase, Setup, Use; the jury-duty waveline uses Summons, Appear, Selection, Trial, Deliberation, Verdict.

**Where the line should start and end.** A run of slides asks where an experience starts and ends: "Start (find it), End (buy it)", then "walk into the store" to "walk out of the store", then "want or need something" to "bought it, used it, finally used it up", then a list running through "Repaired it", "Lent it", "Replaced it", and finally "Customer Dies". The point: chart the whole relationship, not one transaction.

**What goes on the line.** High points carry meanings and positive states; low points carry negative states. On his PC example: Wonder, Truth, Beauty, Freedom, Excitement, Validation, Trust, Discovery, Freedom and Oneness, Comfort above; Frustration, Shock, Anxiety and Fear, Meandering, Boredom below. Opportunities are numbered at specific touchpoints, by customer segment.

**Story shape.** His deck shows the Hero's Journey stages (Ordinary World, Call to Adventure, Refusal, Meeting with the Mentor, Crossing the Threshold, Tests, Allies, and Enemies, Approach to Inmost Cave, Ordeal, Reward, The Road Back, Resurrection, Return with the Elixir) and time-based design tools borrowed from film and music (script, storyboard, beat chart, tempo map, experience blueprint, journey canvas). His ideal jury-duty waveline (a CCA student project, "Jury Delight") "deliberately evokes positive meaning throughout the jury duty experience": the "Path follows a classic narrative arc, is flexible, and depends on behavior to determine outcomes" and the "Cohesive 'story' builds intensity and maintains a consistent rhythm of experience." Its meanings: Validation, Community, Truth, Duty, Justice. Result: "even people who do not serve on a case find the experience meaningful" and "All three personas are satisfied."

**How to draw current versus ideal (the order follows his slides: current line, then ideal line, then touchpoints and opportunities).**
1. Pick one experience and one segment or persona (a research-based portrait of a typical user).
2. Decide where the experience really starts and ends (often earlier and later than the company thinks).
3. Lay out the phases on the time axis.
4. Draw the current line from research, labeling peaks with meanings and dips with negative states.
5. Draw the ideal line on the same axes as a story with build-up, peak, and resolution, and a rhythm, not a flat high line.
6. Mark touchpoints and number the opportunities where the gap is largest.
7. For each opportunity, choose the meaning to deliver and the touchpoint changes.

**What your earlier PawPal waveline got right and what to change.** Your Week 5 slide "Current vs ideal experience" shows an Ideal and a Current line over seven moments ("Need comes up", "Searches around", "Checks if it's trustworthy", "Finds a match", "Meets up or visits", "Shares how it went", "Comes back") grouped into Initiation, Engagement, Conclusion, Continuation.
- Right: current versus ideal on one chart; a continuation phase ("Comes back").

- Change 2: the ideal line sits above the current line everywhere and is nearly flat. His ideal lines follow a narrative arc with rhythm.
- Change 3: the biggest gap on your chart is at "Meets up or visits", which matches your open question "Safety and trust". That is the first opportunity to design and a moment of truth (section 3.1).
- Change 4: his current phase names are Initiation, Immersion, Conclusion, Continuation; "Engagement" is the older middle phase.
- Change 5: the line should come from interview evidence; the deck shows no source for its points.

**Critical take.**
- Strong: puts emotion and meaning into business discussions; the current-versus-ideal gap produces priorities; mixed teams can read it.
- Weak: intensity is subjective; one line averages different people (he answers with lines per segment); drawn from assumptions it is fiction.

### 5.5 They Make Design (2023): What is affordance in UX? (not located)

**What I tried.** They Make Design publishes on Medium (theymakedesign.com redirects to medium.com/theymakedesign). Its main feed and tag feeds ("affordance", "affordances", "ux-design", "ux", "user-experience") show only recent posts, its archive pages are behind Medium's bot check, and its agency sitemap lists no articles. **Background (Wikipedia, verified).** James J. Gibson coined "affordance" in his 1966 book *The Senses Considered as Perceptual Systems*; his best-known definition (1979): "The affordances of the environment are what it offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either for good or ill." In 1988 Donald Norman narrowed it in design to action possibilities a person can readily perceive; in the 2013 edition of *The Design of Everyday Things* he added "signifiers", the cues that show where and how to act.

**Applied to PawPop.** A key box at a front desk affords dropping off a key; a "dog-friendly" decal is a signifier, not an affordance.

### 5.6 Wodtke: Foundations of Interaction Design

**Core idea (verified, full post).** Wodtke describes how she designed CCA's sophomore Foundations class. She argues it is "not enough to teach 'pure' interactive design, you have to teach the skills that support good HCI decisions", drawing on psychology, data and information theory, and implementation. Her core skills: generative research (taught with *Interviewing Users*); concept models ("Designers who make concept models before sitemaps, flows, etc make significantly more coherent work." "Forest, then trees."); information architecture basics (categorization and metadata); interaction design fundamentals (goal-directed design with personas and scenarios, use cases and flows, error states, Fitts's Law, poka-yoke, meaning mistake-proofing); interface design ("Not look and feel, but affordances. Feedback."); and evaluative research. Her "horizontal learnings" practiced all term: sketching; "Iteration with Constant Validation" (research, ideation, research, iteration); "Structured Actionable Critique and Peer Coaching" ("Questions first. Then 3 keeps and changes. Finally ideas to explore."); and "Clarity despite Ambiguity". Projects build skills in a "spiral". Her loudest lesson to students: "ANNOTATE!"

**Note on the date.** Her blog post is dated 14 August 2017; the course cites 2018, possibly a later repost.

**Background that pairs with it (verified on IxDF).** Gillian Crampton Smith's four dimensions of interaction design (1D words, 2D visual representations, 3D physical objects or space, 4D time) and Kevin Silver's fifth, behavior.

**Tool or artifact.** A concept model before screens; annotated wireframes; a critique format.

**Applied to PawPop.** Before wireframing the app map your earlier deck promised, draw a concept model of the things in PawPop's world (owner, dog, place, tip, meetup, trust signal) and how they relate. Annotate every wireframe.

**Critical take.** It is a course-design essay, not a theory; it is most useful as a checklist of what a competent interaction designer covers.

## Week 6: Testing and critique

### 6.1 Moran (2019): Usability testing 101; Mehta (2023, not located)

**Core idea (Moran, verified).**
- "In a usability-testing session, a researcher (called a 'facilitator' or a 'moderator') asks a participant to perform tasks, usually using one or more specific user interfaces."
- Goals: "Identifying problems in the design of the product or service", "Uncovering opportunities to improve", "Learning about the target user's behavior and preferences."
- Elements: facilitator, tasks, participant. Participants are asked to think out loud.
- Types: qualitative or quantitative; remote or in-person; moderated or unmoderated.
- "For a typical qualitative usability study of a single user group, we recommend using five participants to uncover the majority of the most common problems in the product."
- A simple study can run in three days (plan, test five users, analyze); "the cost can run into several hundred thousand dollars for the most elaborate studies."

**Mehta (2023).** Not located (the URL is unknown and the search budget is exhausted). Moran covers the same ground with verified text.

**Applied to PawPop.** Five realistic participants per round from one user group; tasks such as "Find a dog-friendly cafe near you that a local owner recommended this month"; test in context where possible.

**Critical take.** Five users find common problems for one group, not rare problems or differences between groups; usability tests show whether people can use something, not whether they want it.

**Source.** Moran, K. (1 Dec 2019), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/. Confidence: high.

## Concept map: how the concepts form one design process

| Stage | Question | Course tools | Output | Feeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Understand people | Who are they, what do they value, what drives their choices? | Need-finding, qualitative methods, interview planning, 5 kinds of value, core meanings, decision drivers | Segments, drivers, meaning priorities | Stages 2, 3, 4 |
| 2. Understand the system | Who else is involved, and what flows between them? | Stakeholder value map, trends with STEEP, systems thinking | Stakeholder map, trends list | Stage 3 |
| 3. Decide the strategy | Where can we be distinct, and what will we offer? | Market-first sequence, fixed SWOT, continuous strategy | Position, value proposition, offering | Stage 4 |
| 4. Design the experience | What should it feel and mean over time, and where are the make-or-break moments? | Experience dimensions, waveline, moments of truth, service innovation stages | Ideal waveline, moments of truth, service concept | Stages 5, 6 |
| 5. Design the behavior | What exactly must people do, and why don't they? | Fogg B=MAP | Target behaviors, prompts, simplifications | Stage 6 |
| 6. Design the interface | How will people understand and operate it? | Concept models, metaphors, modes, affordances, typography | Prototypes | Stage 7 |
| 7. Test and critique | Does it work, and is it good? | Usability testing, critique, pilots | Findings | Back to stages 1 and 3 |

Shedroff states the link himself: "Value comes from relationships which occur in experiences." Value runs through every stage, and continuous strategy closes the loop.

8. Draw a concept model, prototype, and test with five people per round.
9. Critique with evidence, update the strategy page, repeat.

## Common misreadings to avoid

1. **"Design thinking is a five-step process."** Auernhammer and Roth argue that a step-by-step version "prevents fluency in thinking and flexibility in approach."
2. **"Twelve interviews is always enough."** Only for homogeneous groups and structured guides; about 12 per group when comparing groups.
3. **"Value means price."** Price is one of five kinds.
4. **"Identity and meaning are the same."** Identity asks "Is this me?"; meaning asks "Does this fit into my worldview?"
5. **"The waveline is a journey map."** Shedroff's own slide contrasts them.
6. **"An ideal waveline stays high the whole time."** His ideal lines follow a narrative arc with rhythm.
7. **"Fogg says add motivation."** Fogg says simplify first.
8. **"Ability means skill."** It means simplicity relative to the scarcest resource at that moment.
9. **"There are six ability factors."** The current chain has five; social deviance was dropped.
10. **"Triggers and prompts are different."** Same factor, renamed in late 2017. Shedroff's "triggers" dimension is a different idea.
11. **"A moment of truth is any touchpoint."** It is one of the few emotionally charged interactions; P&G's and Google's terms differ.
12. **"Fixing the SWOT means a better grid."** The fix is validated drivers, market before operations, and the right order; then the SWOT emerges on its own.
13. **"A strength is anything we are good at."** Shedroff: a strength is where you are strong and others are weak.
14. **"Stakeholders are shareholders."** "Stakeholders are different than shareholders."
15. **"More interactive is better."** Each dimension is a choice; the template asks where the experience should sit.
16. **"An affordance is a visual cue."** The cue is a signifier; the affordance is the possible action.
17. **"A modal is just a pop-up."** A mode is any state in which the same input gives a different result.
18. **"Five users is enough for any study."** Five is for qualitative testing with one user group.
19. **"Jones and Samalionis is the Keep the Change case."** It is 1st Source Bank.
20. **"Ireland's chapter is called 'From giving voice to interpreting meaning'."** The published title is "Qualitative Methods: From Boring to Brilliant."

## Ten concrete ways these tools would change how someone works in tech, consulting, or product roles

1. **Write down decision drivers before any roadmap or pitch.** A product manager asks what the buyer actually chooses on, with evidence, instead of listing features.
2. **Separate market issues from operational issues in planning documents.** A consultant builds the deck market-first so "what we are good at" cannot crowd out "what the market rewards".
3. **Size research with evidence.** A researcher budgets about 12 interviews per homogeneous segment and states the conditions in the plan.
4. **Diagnose funnel drop-offs with B=MAP before adding incentives.** A growth team checks prompt timing and the weakest ability link before paying for discounts.
5. **Map value flows on any platform or partnership deal.** A business development lead flags the partner that gives more than it gets as the churn risk.
6. **Add the three qualitative kinds of value to business cases.** A product marketer argues in emotional, identity, and meaningful value as well as cost, and plans research to evidence it.
7. **Rank customer-experience investment by moments of truth.** A customer-experience lead invests first in the few charged moments (an outage, a billing error, a cancellation).
8. **Use current-versus-ideal wavelines to align executives, engineers, and designers.** The tool was built to bridge those groups and turns "improve the experience" into ranked opportunities.
9. **Make testing and critique routine and evidence-based.** Five-person rounds every few sprints; critiques that start with questions and end with written decisions.
10. **Treat strategy as a living document.** A one-page strategy with explicit assumptions, checked against data monthly, with "plot twist" reviews when new facts arrive.

A cross-cutting lesson from Auernhammer and Roth: these tools fail when adopted as ritual without the culture of need-finding, prototyping, and psychological safety that makes them work.
