# Stakeholders around a NYC dog (besides its owner)

Research date: 2026-10-20. Status: COMPLETE for this pass. Method: 23 web searches and 22 page or PDF fetches (a few fetches failed and are noted where relevant). The shared web search budget ran out near the end, so the items listed under "Gaps this report could not close" are unverified and carry no numbers.

## How to read this report

- Every fact carries a source link and the source's publication date. Where a page shows no date, the report says "undated" and gives the date it was checked (2026-10-20).
- Confidence levels: **high** (primary source, recent, direct), **moderate** (reputable secondary source, or older data), **low** (single anecdotal or unclear source). "Unverified" means I could not confirm it and the project should not cite it.
- The 5 kinds of value come from Nathan Shedroff: functional (it works, saves time), financial (money), emotional (feelings), identity (who I am, how others see me), meaning (purpose, a sense that life matters).
- A stakeholder value map shows what each party gives and gets in those 5 kinds of value, plus trust, communication, and data.
- Shorthand used throughout: "vet" means veterinarian, and "vet tech" means veterinary technician (the clinic's nurse-equivalent).

## 1. Dog walkers

**Who they are.** Independent NYC walkers and small walking companies, plus gig walkers who find clients through apps such as Rover and Wag.

**Regulation (almost none).**
- I found no NYC or New York State law that licenses dog walkers or caps how many dogs one walker may take on the street. Confidence: moderate (this rests on an absence of evidence after targeted searches, and secondary guides contradict each other). San Francisco, by contrast, requires a permit for commercial walkers and caps them at 8 dogs (secondary source, moderate).
- Walkers are only covered indirectly. NYC requires every dog to be licensed by the owner, and leashes may be no longer than 6 feet (NYC311, "Unleashed Dog", undated, checked 2026-10-20: https://portal.311.nyc.gov/article/?kanumber=KA-01051). Confidence: moderate.
- After two pit bulls attacked a Chihuahua mix named Penny on the Upper West Side in May 2025 (a town hall drew 500 to 600 people), Assembly Member Jenifer Rajkumar proposed a state bill creating criminal offenses for negligent and reckless dog handling (City & State New York, 2025-08-04: https://www.cityandstateny.com/politics/2025/08/how-nyc-governs-dogs/407159/). A Senate bill on the same subject, S8293A, called "Penny's law", was introduced 2025-05-30 (https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S8293). Confidence: high for the bills' existence; I could not confirm whether either passed.

**Pay and platform fees.**

| Platform | What the walker keeps | Source and date | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rover | 80% of the booking in most US states (Rover takes 20%; 25% in California). Owners also pay Rover a separate booking fee. | Secondary fee guides citing Rover's help center, 2026 (https://www.pupline.app/blog/how-much-does-rover-take) | moderate |
| Wag | Roughly 60%. Wag does not publish a percentage; its help center says owners pay about 1.67 times the walker's rate, which implies a cut near 40%. | Secondary guides, 2026 (https://www.lilachbullock.com/how-much-does-wag-pay/) | low to moderate |

**Risks: lost dogs, deaths, and thin training.**
- In January 2025, a Queens owner's 3-month-old puppy disappeared from a stroller in Kissena Corridor Park while with a Rover "Star Sitter" who had passed Rover's enhanced background check. Rover called such events "extremely infrequent" but gave no frequency data. The Rover Guarantee covers up to $25,000 in vet costs but excludes "stolen property". A former walker who organizes for Voters For Animal Rights said gig walkers get very little formal training (Gothamist, 2025-01-15: https://gothamist.com/news/a-queens-woman-trusted-a-star-sitter-on-rover-then-her-dog-went-missing). Confidence: high.
- Wag walkers lost or killed several NYC dogs between 2015 and 2019, including Duckie in Prospect Park in 2015 (ABC7 New York: https://abc7ny.com/post/brooklyn-women-blame-wag-app-for-the-death-of-their-dog-duckie/1106715/) and Norman on the Upper East Side in 2018 (PIX11: https://pix11.com/news/local-news/manhattan/dog-walker-from-uber-for-dogs-app-loses-new-york-couples-chihuahua/). The New York Post reported in 2019 that a Yorkshire terrier killed in Hudson Yards was at least the 11th dog lost or killed by Wag walkers in NYC since 2015 (as quoted at https://appellate-brief.com/dogs-killed-during-wag-walks/). Confidence: moderate (the 11-dog count is the Post's, relayed by a law-firm page).
- I found no public data on bite injuries to walkers. Treat walker injury rates as unknown.

**What walkers wish owners knew (interview hypotheses, not sourced).** I found no survey of walkers' wishes. Based on the incidents above, test these in walker interviews: owners under-report reactivity, escape habits, and bite history; owners pick the cheapest app walker for a difficult dog; owners expect photo-perfect reports but never share the dog's vet or behavior notes.

**Value exchange with owners (5 kinds of value).**

| Value | Walker gives | Walker gets |
|---|---|---|
| Functional | Exercise, potty breaks, eyes on the dog during the workday | Steady routes, keys or building access |
| Financial | A service priced per walk | Income, minus a 20% to 40% platform cut on apps |
| Emotional | Peace of mind for the owner | Affection from dogs; also blame and fear when something goes wrong |
| Identity | Lets the owner be a "good owner" while working long hours | Little professional status; no license or credential to signal skill |
| Meaning | The dog's daily joy | Pride in animal care, mostly invisible to clients |

**Trust, communication, data.** Trust runs mostly one way: owners hand over a key and a family member, but the only verification on apps is a background check. Walk reports (GPS maps, photos, "pee/poo" checkboxes) are the main data flow. Nothing travels back to the walker about the dog's behavior history, recent vet visits, or reactivity, which is exactly what a walker needs to stay safe. This gap is an inference from the sources above, not a measured finding.

**Partnership potential.** High. Walkers see each dog more often than anyone except the owner. They are a natural channel for behavior and health observations, and they are poorly served by app platforms.

## 2. Daycare and boarding operators and staff

**Who they are.** Facilities that host dogs by day (daycare) or overnight (boarding), and the playroom staff who supervise group play. (I found no verified data on these workers' pay or age; treat that as an interview question.)

**Regulation.**
- **NYC does regulate the premises.** NYC Health Code section 161.09 requires a Health Department permit to operate a pet shop, grooming parlor, boarding kennel, or training establishment. The Health Code's definition of a boarding kennel appears broad enough to cover daycare (moderate confidence: I saw the definition only in summary). A supervising manager must pass the city's Animal Care and Handling Course, and the facility must pass an inspection before it opens (NYC MyCity Business, "Small Animal Boarding Establishment Permit", undated, checked 2026-10-20: https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/description/small-animal-boarding-establishment-permit; Health Code text: https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/newyorkcity/latest/NYCrules/0-0-0-47116). Confidence: high for the permit; moderate for fee details, which sources report inconsistently.
- **New York State has no statewide licensing or inspection system for boarding facilities** (News 12 Long Island, 2026-08-27: https://longisland.news12.com/2026/08/27/pet-boarding-facilities-have-little-oversight-in-ny-state/4edHsDtPn42ugtDxQ6Qw4M). Confidence: high. A Long Island owner whose German shepherd died at a Wantagh facility in August 2026 said: "Apparently anybody walking down the street can open up their own doggy day care or boarding facility."
- **Pending state bill.** The bipartisan NYS Safe Pet Boarding Act (S8510 / A9123, introduced September 2025) would require annual state licenses, inspections, staff training, and record keeping. It followed the deaths of 21 dogs left without enough water in an unventilated enclosure during a heatwave at a boarder in Argyle, Washington County. As of August 2026 it sat in the Agriculture Committee with unclear prospects (https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S8510; News 12 above). Confidence: moderate to high.
- **Neighboring precedent.** Massachusetts signed "Ollie's Law" on 2024-09-20, named for a 7-month-old labradoodle mauled by other dogs at a boarding facility in 2020. It requires kennel inspections and reporting of injuries, and directs the state to write rules on staff ratios and group sizes. Its advisory committee discussed 1 staff per 15 dogs; as of spring 2026, the rules were still in draft (Mass.gov: https://www.mass.gov/info-details/ollies-law-information; MSPCA-Angell: https://www.mspca.org/animal_protection/ollieslaw/). Confidence: moderate to high.

**Industry standards.**
- The **Pet Care Services Association no longer exists.** It was the renamed American Boarding Kennel Association and reportedly filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy in September 2011 (Pet Product News, date not shown: https://www.petproductnews.com/archives/abka-changes-name-to-pet-care-services-association/article_4278b43c-bac0-530c-97bb-06b2a154b5c1.html, plus secondary summaries). Confidence: moderate. The current trade body is the International Boarding & Pet Services Association (IBPSA).
- The commonly cited playgroup ratio is **1 staff member per 10 to 15 dogs**. Colorado and Iowa allow up to 15 dogs per person, and North Carolina requires 1 person per 10 dogs in a shared area (secondary industry sources, 2026). Confidence: moderate.
- Temperament testing ("evaluation") is voluntary practice, not law. The Professional Animal Care Certification Council (PACCC) certifies staff on topics such as reading play styles, pre-screening owner questionnaires, and when to stop a dog-to-dog introduction (https://paccert.org/). Confidence: moderate.

**Value exchange and flows.** Owners give money (functional value: a tired dog and a free workday) and the identity of being a "responsible" owner whose dog socializes. Operators get recurring revenue but carry the risk of fights and injuries. Trust is low-information: owners usually see a webcam, a report card, and the outcome of an evaluation they cannot audit. In Massachusetts, injury reporting became a legal duty only in 2024; in New York there is no public injury data at all.

**Partnership potential.** Moderate. Operators want fewer bad-fit dogs and fewer incidents; they need better behavior history from owners before intake.

## 3. Groomers

**Licensing.**
- **No US state licenses individual dog groomers** (secondary industry guides, 2026). Confidence: moderate. NYC licenses the **premises** through the Small Animal Grooming Establishment Permit, with the same certified-manager and inspection rules as boarding (https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/description/small-animal-grooming-establishment-permit). Confidence: high. No one tests whether the person holding the clippers is competent.
- **New York bills that died.** A1108 (Assembly Member Paulin, introduced 2025-01-09) would have licensed grooming businesses through the Secretary of State; its memo states "there have been several cases in New York of healthy dogs dying while in the care of grooming facilities." S6963 (Senator Lanza, introduced 2025-03-27) would have registered individual groomers for $40 a year with training and testing. Neither passed in the 2025-2026 session (https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/A1108; https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2025/S6963). Confidence: moderate.
- **New Jersey's "Bijou's Law"** has been introduced in every session since 2014, after a 6-year-old dog named Bijou died during grooming. It passed the full Assembly 68-5-2 in 2018 (A3044) but stalled in the Senate. The current version, S387, was introduced 2026-01-13 and is in committee (https://legiscan.com/NJ/bill/S387/2026; https://pub.njleg.gov/Bills/2018/A3500/3044_R1.HTM). Confidence: moderate.

**Injuries and deaths.**
- A 2018 NJ Advance Media investigation documented 47 dog deaths during or shortly after grooming in 14 states since 2008; 20 involved flat-faced breeds and 9 were in New Jersey (reported in secondary sources; original not fetched). Confidence: moderate.
- In 2023 a Queens groomer at a Flushing salon was charged with aggravated animal cruelty after a 4-year-old Maltese died; prosecutors cited surveillance video (NBC New York: https://www.nbcnewyork.com/news/local/crime-and-courts/queens-dog-groomer-charged-with-animal-abuse-after-maltese-dies-during-session/4368557/). Confidence: moderate.
- In January 2026, a bulldog died at a Pequannock, New Jersey groomer about 90 minutes after arriving (Daily Voice: https://dailyvoice.com/article/pequannock-groomer-grieves-too-after-5-year-old-bulldog-drops-dead-during-appointment/). Confidence: moderate.

**Value and flows.** The owner sees only the finished dog, not the 2 to 4 hours behind a closed door. Trust rests on reputation and reviews. Data that would protect the dog (heart or breathing conditions, matting history, anxiety, past nicks) flows by memory or not at all. Groomers also absorb the identity work: owners judge them on looks, while the groomer manages risk.

**Partnership potential.** Moderate. Good groomers want calmer dogs, honest medical history, and a way to prove skill in a field with no license.

## 4. Trainers

**Regulation and credentials.**
- No US state requires a license to work as a dog trainer. The Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers (CCPDT) itself describes the industry as unregulated (CCPDT, "How to choose a dog trainer", undated: https://www.ccpdt.org/dog-owners/how-to-choose-a-dog-trainer/). Confidence: high.
- NYC requires a Health Department permit for a training **establishment** (a facility), but in-home and park trainers need none (https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/description/small-animal-training-establishment-permit/apply). Confidence: moderate.
- Voluntary credentials include CCPDT's CPDT-KA (300+ training hours in 3 years plus an exam), CPDT-KSA (skills, by video), and CBCC-KA (behavior consulting); others come from IAABC (behavior consultants), Karen Pryor Academy, and the Association of Professional Dog Trainers (APDT), which is a membership body rather than a certifier. Confidence: moderate.

**The methods debate.**
- The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) 2021 position statement recommends only reward-based training and states: "There is no evidence that aversive methods are more effective than reward-based methods in any context." AVSAB defines aversive methods as those that rely on force, pain, or physical or emotional discomfort, such as shock (e-collars), prong, and choke collars (AVSAB, 2021: https://avsab.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/AVSAB-Humane-Dog-Training-Position-Statement-2021.pdf). Confidence: high.
- CCPDT replaced its older "Least Intrusive, Minimally Aversive" (LIMA) policy with a Prohibited Practices Policy in 2025. It bans alpha rolls and pinning, and bans e-collars for dogs under 1 year and for fear or aggression cases, but still leaves room for some aversive tools. A petition from certified trainers objects that prong, slip, and e-collars remain allowed (https://www.ccpdt.org/about-us/ethics-and-professional-conduct/prohibited-practices-policy/; https://www.change.org/p/certified-trainers-demand-humane-standards-end-ccpdt-s-support-of-harmful-tools). Confidence: moderate.

**Why owners distrust trainers (inference from the evidence above).** There is no license to check, "certified" can mean very different things, the profession's own bodies disagree on whether shock and prong collars are acceptable, and the most visible trainers on social media sell fast results. An owner cannot tell from a title whether a trainer will hurt the dog. I did not find a survey that measures owner distrust directly; treat this as a reasoned hypothesis for interviews.

**Value and flows.** Trainers sell functional value (a dog that behaves), emotional relief, and identity (a "well-behaved dog" makes the owner look competent in a dense city). Their success depends on owner homework they never see. The trainer rarely receives data from the vet (pain can cause aggression) or the walker (what triggers the dog on the street).

**Partnership potential.** High for behavior-focused projects. Reward-based trainers want owners to practice between sessions and want referrals from vets.

## 5. Vets and vet techs

**Mental health and suicide risk.**
- **Distress.** In Merck Animal Health's fourth wellbeing study (survey fall 2023, released January 2024, done with the AVMA), 10% of veterinarians met the threshold for serious psychological distress, compared with 6.3% of employed, college-educated US adults. Among other practice staff, such as technicians, the rate was 20%. About 6.7% of veterinarians reported suicidal ideation, compared with 4.5% of the general population. Use of mental health treatment rose from 15% in 2019 to 25% (Merck press release, 2024-01: https://www.merck-animal-health-usa.com/newsroom/merck-animal-healths-fourth-veterinary-wellbeing-study-indicates-progress-in-addressing-mental-health-challenges-among-veterinary-teams/). Confidence: moderate to high.
- **Suicide deaths.** A CDC study of 11,620 veterinarian deaths from 1979 to 2015 found female veterinarians were 3.5 times and male veterinarians 2.1 times as likely to die by suicide as the general population (CDC press release, 2018-12-20; JAVMA 2019: https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/media/releases/2018/p1220-veterinarians-suicide.html). A second study of 2003 to 2014 deaths found suicide ratios of 5.0 for male and 2.3 for female veterinary technicians, the first evidence that techs share the risk (CDC NIOSH, 2019: https://cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2019/suicide-veterinarians.html). Confidence: high (the death records are 11 to 47 years old).
- **Compassion fatigue.** 88% of 1,404 respondents to the National Association of Veterinary Technicians in America (NAVTA) 2024 survey (released 2025-04-03) have had compassion fatigue (emotional exhaustion from caring for suffering animals and their people). Low pay was the top challenge for 39%; the average hourly rate was $32.89 (https://navta.net/news/navtas-2024-demographic-survey-reveals-strong-feelings-on-title-protection-income-role-clarity-and-wellness/). Confidence: moderate to high.
- **Turnover.** The American Animal Hospital Association (AAHA) reports average annual turnover of 23% among veterinary team members (https://www.aaha.org/trends-magazine/publications/does-your-practice-have-a-turnover-problem/, date not confirmed). Confidence: moderate.

**Client aggression.**
- A US survey of clinical veterinarians recruited through the Veterinary Information Network (American Journal of Veterinary Research, 2026, ahead of print; about 1,100 respondents per the abstract summary I saw) found 98.9% had been verbally abused by a client during their career, 38.2% had been physically threatened, and 34.5% had considered leaving the profession because of verbal abuse (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42335953/). Confidence: moderate (the sample came from an online veterinary network and may over-represent people with strong views).
- In a 2022 US survey of 551 support staff, mostly technicians, 54.1% had been verbally assaulted by a client in the previous 6 months, and 77.9% felt management was too slow to fire difficult clients (Veterinary Medicine and Science, 2022: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/vms3.725). Confidence: moderate.

**What vets wish owners did differently, and the communication gap.**
- **Cost drives skipped care.** In the PetSmart Charities and Gallup study (2,498 dog and cat owners, surveyed late 2024 to January 2025, released April 2025), 52% skipped or declined recommended vet care in the past year, and 71% of those cited cost (https://news.gallup.com/poll/659057/pet-owners-skipped-declined-veterinary-care.aspx). Confidence: high.
- **Vets and owners remember the same conversation differently.** 73% of owners who declined care for cost said the vet offered no cheaper option, and only 23% recall ever being offered a payment plan. Yet in Gallup's fall 2025 survey of 933 vets, 81% said they always or often recommend an alternative plan; 94% said client finances often or sometimes block recommended treatment; only 51% often or always practice "spectrum of care" (offering a range of options from basic and cheaper to advanced and costlier); and 48% said their training did not prepare them to discuss financial barriers with clients (https://news.gallup.com/poll/700115/veterinarians-say-cost-main-driver-declined-care.aspx). Confidence: high. This is one of the clearest stakeholder-map findings in this report. The communication channel between vet and owner fails at the exact moment money enters.
- **Preventive care gaps.** In the AVMA's 2025 Pet Ownership and Demographics Sourcebook, 16.1% of dog owners and 28.0% of cat owners spent nothing on vet care in the past year (as reported in AVMA coverage, https://www.avma.org/news/evolving-pet-owner-economics-what-data-reveal-veterinary-teams). Confidence: moderate.
- **Is there a vet shortage?** Contested. An AVMA-commissioned 2024 analysis concluded that existing vet schools likely produce enough veterinarians to meet demand through 2035; the well-documented shortages are in rural practice and in credentialed technicians who leave (secondary summary). Confidence: low to moderate.

**Value and flows.** Vets give functional value (health), emotional value (relief, and grief support at euthanasia), and meaning. They get money, but also blame for rising prices and, as shown above, verbal abuse. Data flows are fragmented: records sit in each clinic's software, and walkers, groomers, daycares, and trainers rarely see vaccination or behavior notes, although NYC boarding and grooming permits require proof of vaccination.

**Partnership potential.** High, if the project reduces friction (pre-visit history, cost conversations, low-stress handling) instead of adding another app the clinic must monitor.

## 6. Shelters and rescues

**Who they are.** Animal Care Centers of NYC (ACC), the city's contracted open-admission shelter (it must accept every animal brought in), plus private rescues and the ASPCA.

**The capacity crisis.**
- In July 2025, ACC suspended intake for the first time in its 30-year history, with more than 1,000 animals in its care. ACC said: "We have never had this many animals in our care before. This isn't a drill. This is a crisis" (NBC New York, 2025-07-21: https://www.nbcnewyork.com/clear-the-shelters/nyc-animal-care-centers-suspend-intake-pets-surrender/6342360/). City & State reported more than 380 dogs in care at that point (2025-08-04, link in section 1). Confidence: high.
- **One month of data (July 2026, ACC's own report).** 544 dogs came in (363 strays, 181 surrendered by owners). 456 left alive (157 adopted, 107 returned to owners, 192 transferred to other groups), 68 were euthanized, and 2 died in care. Inflow exceeded outflow by 18 dogs in a single month. Cats were the larger group: 1,025 cats came in, including 325 owner surrenders (ACC Shelter Animals Count report, July 2026: https://www.nycacc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/ACC-2026-JUL-SAC.pdf). Confidence: high.
- **Length of stay.** ACC has said dogs stay 15 or more days on average, against an ideal of 5 to 7 days for an open-admission shelter, and that the average was about 7 days in 2019 (reported in 2024 to 2026 coverage summarized by search; I did not open the primary page). Confidence: low to moderate.
- **Who waits longest.** Nationally, 2.8 million dogs entered US shelters and rescues in 2025; the dog adoption rate rose to 57%; about 320,000 dogs were euthanized; large dogs still have the longest median stays (Shelter Animals Count 2025 annual report, released 2026-02-04: https://www.shelteranimalscount.org/2025-report; ASPCA release: https://www.aspca.org/about-us/press-releases/shelter-animals-count-releases-2025-annual-data-report-marking-10-years). Confidence: moderate to high. ACC's emergency foster appeal asks for homes for dogs of 40 to 80 pounds for at least 2 weeks (https://www.nycacc.org/emergency-fosters/, undated, checked 2026-10-20). Confidence: high.

**Adopter support after adoption.** A 2025 peer-reviewed study notes that up to 20% of adopted pets are returned, and found no significant difference in return rates between dogs whose adopters attended post-adoption training (8.3%) and those who did not (9.3%) (Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2025: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159125001522). Confidence: moderate. Training classes alone do not seem to hold adoptions together; what does is an open question worth interviewing adopters about.

**Staff burnout.** I did not verify shelter-specific burnout data in this pass. ACC received $1 million from the mayor in 2025, partly to hire 14 more staff, and more than $10.6 million in ASPCA grants over time (search summaries of ASPCA and city announcements; primary pages not opened). Confidence: low to moderate.

**Value and flows.** Shelters give meaning (a life saved) and identity ("I rescued") to adopters and fosters. They need fosters, adopters, and donations. Communication is mostly one-way appeals ("we are out of space"). Data: ACC publishes monthly outcome reports, which is unusually transparent, but behavior notes on each dog rarely travel with the dog after adoption.

**Partnership potential.** Very high. ACC has ready-made, low-commitment programs for non-owners (section 12) and an urgent, measurable need.

## 7. Buildings: landlords, co-op boards, supers, doormen

**The NYC Pet Law makes building staff legally important.**
- Under NYC Administrative Code section 27-2009.1 (1983), in a building with 3 or more units, if a tenant keeps a pet openly for 3 months with the knowledge of the landlord or the landlord's agent, and the landlord does not start legal action, the lease's no-pet clause is waived for that animal. Public housing is excluded, and a pet that is a nuisance or damages the premises can still be grounds for eviction (NYC Mayor's Office of Animal Welfare, "Animals and Housing, Evictions", undated, checked 2026-10-20: https://www.nyc.gov/site/animalwelfare/resources/evictions.page; NYC Bar guide, about 2009: https://www2.nycbar.org/Publications/pdf/CompanionAnimals_NYC%20Apartments09.pdf). Confidence: high.
- **Doormen and supers count as the landlord's eyes.** In Seward Park Housing Corp. v. Cohen (Appellate Division, First Department, 2001), the court held that building employees' knowledge of a pet is imputed to (legally treated as) the landlord's knowledge, and that the law applies to co-ops (https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ny-supreme-court-appellate-division/1168315.html). NYC's guidance tells tenants to collect evidence that the pet was kept openly, such as vet records, letters from neighbors, sightings in the lobby, and pet-supply deliveries (NYC Animal Welfare page above). Confidence: moderate to high. Condos may be treated differently by court district (NYC Bar guide, about 2009; may be outdated). Confidence: low.
- **Evictions.** City marshals must check for animals and leave notice of which shelter received any animal removed (NYC Animal Welfare page above). Confidence: high.

**Doormen as an informal dog-care network.**
- Doormen commonly hold keys for dog walkers so owners do not have to wait at home (Brick Underground, 2014: https://www.brickunderground.com/blog/2014/08/buy_curious_doorman_buildings_vs_non_doorman_buildings). Confidence: moderate. A 2026 guide for new NYC walking businesses calls doormen "your distribution channel" and advises holiday tips (https://nuzzo.pet/guides/start-a-dog-walking-business/new-york). Confidence: low to moderate.
- **The trust gap shows when it fails.** In 2026, a Long Island City building's management said a doorman gave a vacationing couple's keys to a woman posing as their dog walker, without verifying who she was (FOX 5 New York, 2026, exact date not confirmed: https://www.fox5ny.com/news/nyc-couple-finds-stranger-in-apartment). Confidence: moderate.
- Doormen receive no formal role, pay, or information in this network. Columbia sociologist Peter Bearman's book "Doormen" (2005) studied the tenant-doorman relationship in depth and could inform interviews (not opened in this pass).

**Noise complaints.** The NYC Noise Code allows enforcement when animal noise is "plainly audible" from nearby homes for 10 continuous minutes between 7 a.m. and 10 p.m., or 5 continuous minutes between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. First complaints usually lead to education mailed to the owner (NYC Department of Environmental Protection, "A Guide to New York City's Noise Code", printed 03/2018: https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dep/downloads/pdf/air/noise/noise-code-guide-summary.pdf). Confidence: high. Barking is mostly a symptom of a dog left alone too long, which links buildings back to walkers and daycare.

**Pet fees and deposits.** I did not verify New York's rules on pet deposits in this pass (a 2019 state law caps security deposits at one month's rent, which secondary guides say limits extra pet deposits). Treat as unverified.

**Value and flows.** Landlords and boards get rent and fewer complaints; they give permission (or tolerance). Doormen give access, eyes, and goodwill; they get tips and affection, but carry risk when something goes wrong. Communication is informal and verbal. Data barely exists: no building knows which dogs live there, who walks them, or who holds keys.

**Partnership potential.** Moderate to high. A building-level solution (who walks which dog, verified handoffs, a list of dogs in case of fire) serves owners, walkers, doormen, and boards at once.

## 8. Businesses: restaurants, cafes, retailers, hotels, offices

**Restaurants and cafes.**
- New York State has allowed dogs in restaurants' outdoor dining areas, at the owner's discretion, since a 2015 law. Indoors, NYC's Health Code bans live animals in food service establishments except service dogs and police dogs; restaurants can be fined up to $250 (NYC311, "Dogs in Restaurants", undated: https://portal.311.nyc.gov/article/?kanumber=KA-02689; NYC Health Code section 81.25: https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/newyorkcity/latest/NYCrules/0-0-0-46291). Confidence: moderate to high.
- **Complaints are rising fast.** 311 complaints about dogs in NYC bakeries, groceries, and restaurants more than doubled, from 683 in 2023 to 1,525 in 2025, with more than 700 from January 1 to June 1, 2026. One Upper East Side bakery drew 583 complaints since 2020. A Brooklyn Heights barista said: "It's really a huge burden, because so many people are bringing their dogs in." A customer argued that a lack of pet accommodation "might make or break a restaurant." Darin Detwiler of Northeastern University told the paper that "the current system is being abused" (New York Post, 2026-06-03, via Yahoo: https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/dogs-barking-trouble-nyc-eateries-230501299.html). Confidence: high.
- In June 2025, the NYC Health Department proposed updating its rules on dogs in outdoor dining to cover the city's new year-round sidewalk and seasonal roadway cafes; I found no record that it was adopted (https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doh/downloads/pdf/notice/2025/noi-ch32-dogs-dining-areas-06-18-25.pdf). Confidence: moderate.

**Retailers, hotels, offices.** I did not verify data on NYC retail, hotel, or office dog policies in this pass. Treat any claim about them as unverified and use interviews (for example, ask a hotel front desk or an office manager how often dogs cause complaints).

**Value and flows.** Businesses that welcome dogs get foot traffic, loyalty, and an identity as "neighborhood" places. They carry the cost: staff stress, complaints from customers who fear or dislike dogs, and inspection risk. Communication between owner and business is a guess at the door. There is no shared signal for "this dog is calm and welcome here" versus "this is a fake service dog".

**Partnership potential.** Moderate. Dog-friendly cafes are natural venues for community events; they need clear norms more than they need apps.

## 9. Neighbors who do not own dogs

**Bites (the hardest numbers in this report).** From the NYC Health Department's "Understanding Dog Bites to New York City Residents" (NYC Vital Signs, April 2026: https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doh/downloads/pdf/survey/dog-bites-2026.pdf). Confidence: high.
- 3,814 reported bites to NYC residents in 2024, the highest of the 10 years from 2015 to 2024, or 45 per 100,000 residents. Staten Island had the highest rate (77 per 100,000), and the rate was 1.2 times higher in very-high-poverty neighborhoods than in low-poverty ones.
- 29% of bites happened when the victim was simply passing by or entering the dog's space. 31% came from a dog in the victim's own building or household.
- Among bites outside the victim's home, 10% were from unleashed dogs and 7% from leashed but uncontrolled dogs.
- **The follow-up system is broken.** 39% of reported bites lacked a complete address or phone number for the dog's owner. Of owners who could be contacted, only 50% answered the Health Department about the dog's health during the required 10-day observation. As a result, victims get rabies shots they likely do not need: the share receiving them rose from 7% in 2015 to 11% in 2024, although NYC has not had a rabid dog in over 60 years.
- Bites are likely under-reported, and licensed dogs (which would make owners traceable) are declining every year.
- A Council bill introduced 2026-03-26 (Int. 0783-2026, lead sponsor Gale Brewer) would create an online dog-bite report form and train 311 and police staff (https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=7961415&GUID=9ACD33F1-AEED-4ED7-B760-3DD4130DE6A5). Confidence: moderate (status: in committee as of the last listing I saw).

**Waste.** 311 complaints about dog waste have risen every year since the pandemic, yet the city issued just 2 summonses for failing to remove dog waste in the previous year, despite a fine of up to $250. Four Council bills introduced in 2026 would fill bag dispensers, compost dog-run waste, post signs, and run an education campaign (Healthbeat and THE CITY, 2026-05-01: https://www.healthbeat.org/newyork/2026/05/01/dog-poop-scoop-city-council/; City & State, 2025-08-04). Confidence: high.

**Noise and parks.** See the Noise Code limits in section 7. NYC has 91 dog runs; the Parks Department builds new ones only when a community group commits to maintenance (City & State, 2025-08-04). Confidence: high.

**Fear of dogs.** I did not verify the prevalence of fear of dogs (cynophobia) in this pass. Treat it as an interview topic, not a number.

**Value and flows.** Neighbors give tolerance and shared space; they get almost nothing back except, sometimes, the social warmth of a friendly dog. They bear real costs (bites, waste, noise) with weak enforcement. Trust between non-owners and owners is low and is damaged by a small share of careless owners. The data flow that would protect a bite victim (who owns this dog, is it vaccinated) fails about half the time.

**Partnership potential.** Low as customers, high as a design constraint. Any project that puts more dogs in more places must answer to this group.

## 10. Cat owners

**Vet under-use and visit stress.**
- In the Bayer Veterinary Care Usage Study with the American Association of Feline Practitioners (2013, published in JAVMA 2014), 52% of cats had not had a checkup in the past year. 58% of cat owners said their cat hates going to the vet, and 38% said just thinking about a visit stressed them out. 81% saw cats as independent and low-need (AAHA NEWStat, 2013-07: https://www.aaha.org/publications/newstat/articles/2013-07/bayer-aafp-study-explains-why-52-percent-of-cat-owners-avoid-vet-visits/). Confidence: moderate (the surveys ran from 2011 to 2013, and I found no newer edition).
- More recently, 28.0% of cat owners versus 16.1% of dog owners spent nothing on vet care in the past year (AVMA 2025 Pet Ownership and Demographics Sourcebook, via AVMA coverage). Confidence: moderate.

**Cats dominate the shelter problem in NYC.** In July 2026, cats made up 1,025 of ACC's 1,611 intakes (64%), and cat owner surrenders (325) outnumbered dog owner surrenders (181) (ACC report, section 6). Confidence: high. Yet city attention is dog-shaped: 91 dog runs, dog licensing, and dog bills.

**Isolation: no walk, fewer chance meetings.**
- In a four-city study (data from 2012), pet owners were more likely than non-owners to get to know people in their neighborhood, and dog owners were about 5 times as likely as other pet owners to do so (odds ratio 5.01). About 40% of pet owners got some form of social support from people they met through their pet (Wood et al., "The Pet Factor", PLOS ONE, 2015: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0122085). Confidence: high.
- A study of older US pet owners found dog owners were 2.4 times as likely as cat owners to meet neighbors, and dog walking is the mechanism (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6845441/). Confidence: moderate. The evidence is mixed: a study of older adults in Japan linked cat ownership to more interaction with neighbors (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0206399). Confidence: moderate.
- In plain terms, cat owners are not proven to be lonelier, but they lack the daily public ritual that turns dog owners into a visible community. Whether NYC cat owners feel isolated is a question for interviews.

**Cat cafes.** Meow Parlour, at 43 Essex Street on the Lower East Side, opened in 2014 as NYC's first cat cafe. It operates as a licensed nonprofit rescue: every cat is adoptable, reservations are recommended, and it recruits fosters (https://www.meowparlour.com/, undated, checked 2026-10-20). Confidence: high. I did not verify other NYC cat cafes in this pass.

**Travel and indoor boredom.** I found no verified data on cat sitters during travel or on indoor enrichment for NYC apartment cats. Treat both as interview topics.

**Value and flows.** Cat owners get strong emotional value at home, but little identity or community value in public. They give money to vets less often, and the vet relationship is weakest exactly where cats hide illness. Data flows are thin: a cat with no annual visit has no current record anywhere.

**Partnership potential.** High, and under-explored. Meow Parlour and ACC's cat foster programs are ready partners.

## 11. Elderly and disabled owners

**Programs that help them keep pets.**
- PAWS NY says it helps "New York City's most vulnerable residents remain with their pets" through volunteer dog walking and cat care, a pet food pantry, a veterinary care program, vet transportation, and foster and emergency pet care (https://www.pawsny.org/, undated, checked 2026-10-20). Confidence: high for the services; the site gave no client or volunteer counts.
- At the city level, Council Member Bob Holden proposed a pet food pantry pilot, and Council Member Shaun Abreu proposed expanding paid leave to cover pet care (City & State, 2025-08-04). Confidence: high for the proposals; I did not confirm whether they passed.
- Meals on Wheels pet programs: I could not verify a current NYC program in this pass. Treat as unverified.

**When the owner is hospitalized, evicted, or dies.**
- NYC requires marshals to check for animals during evictions and leave notice of which shelter took them (NYC Animal Welfare page, section 7). Confidence: high.
- PAWS NY lists foster and emergency pet care (above). I found no verified NYC data on how many pets enter shelters because an owner was hospitalized or died. New York law allows a "pet trust" to fund a pet's care after death (Estates, Powers and Trusts Law 7-8.1); unverified in this pass.
- I could not load the University of Michigan National Poll on Healthy Aging report on pets (2019, server returned an error), so its figures on older adults and pets are unverified here.

**Value and flows.** For older owners, the pet often carries the deepest meaning value in this whole map: a reason to get up, routine, and companionship. The risk is concentrated in one point of failure, the owner's health. Communication between hospitals, home-care workers, and pet-care helpers barely exists. No one records "this patient has a dog alone at home."

**Partnership potential.** High for a meaningful project. PAWS NY's volunteer model is a direct fit for students.

## 12. Children and families; people who love dogs but cannot own one

**Children.**
- Children are bitten at higher rates. In 2014 data, the reported bite rate among NYC children 17 and younger was 1.5 times the adult rate (49.5 versus 32.0 per 100,000), and children aged 5 to 12 had the highest rate (61.5 per 100,000). Bites to children more often involved a dog from the child's own household (28% versus 21% for adults) and happened in the child's home (29% versus 19%). Among children 12 and younger, 32% of reported bites happened while playing with or near a dog (NYC Vital Signs, July 2017: https://www.nyc.gov/assets/doh/downloads/pdf/survey/survey-dog-bites.pdf). Confidence: high, but the data are 12 years old; the 2026 report could not update age patterns because age was missing in 20% of 2024 reports.
- The Health Department's current advice to owners: "supervise all child-dog interactions" and give the dog a quiet retreat space (NYC Vital Signs, April 2026, section 9). Confidence: high.

**People who love dogs but cannot own one (students, renters, travelers).**
- **ACC BoroughBreaks:** take a shelter dog on a day trip lasting "from one hour to all day" (hikes, beach trips, a nap at home). No orientation is required, and appointment slots are released weekly and "fill quickly". Offered at the Manhattan, Queens, and Staten Island centers (https://www.nycacc.org/foster/, undated, checked 2026-10-20). Confidence: high.
- **ACC Straycations:** short fosters of at least 3 days, after an orientation. Standard fostering asks 3 to 4 weeks. **Renters need landlord permission to foster a dog**, which blocks exactly the people (renters, students) who cannot own (same page). Confidence: high.
- BorrowMyDoggy is a UK platform that matches dog owners with local people who want time with a dog; its homepage did not show member counts when checked (https://www.borrowmydoggy.com/, checked 2026-10-20). Confidence: low on scale.

**Value and flows.** Non-owners can give exactly what the system lacks: walking time, foster nights, and company for dogs whose owners are old, sick, or overworked. In return they get emotional value and meaning without the 10-to-15-year commitment. The connection is broken by trust (who is this stranger?), permission (landlords), and information (which dog needs what, when).

**Partnership potential.** Very high. This group is large around Columbia, and ACC and PAWS NY already have programs that need them.

## Top 10 findings that should shape the project

1. **The vet-owner cost conversation fails at the moment money enters.** 52% of owners skipped or declined recommended care in the past year, 71% of them because of cost. 73% of cost-decliners say the vet offered no cheaper option, while 81% of vets say they always or often do (Gallup, 2025). A project here serves owners, vets, and animals at once. Confidence: high.
2. **Every professional around the dog works without the dog's history.** Walkers, groomers, daycares, and trainers rarely receive health and behavior information, and the incidents in this report (lost dogs, grooming deaths, daycare fights) happen where that information is missing. Confidence: moderate (this is a pattern across sources, not a measured statistic).
3. **Almost none of these jobs is licensed at the person level in New York.** NYC permits the premises of boarding, grooming, and training businesses, but no one tests the individual. The state has no boarding oversight, and the 2025-2026 grooming and boarding bills stalled. Owners choose on reviews and luck. Confidence: moderate to high.
4. **Non-owners carry measurable costs.** 3,814 reported bites in 2024 (a 10-year high), 29% to people simply passing by; dog-waste complaints rising with 2 summonses in a year; complaints about dogs inside food shops up from 683 in 2023 to 1,525 in 2025. Any idea that puts more dogs in more places must answer to them. Confidence: high.
5. **Cats are the hidden majority of the problem.** Cats were 64% of ACC intake in July 2026, cat owner surrenders outnumbered dog owner surrenders, and cat owners skip the vet more. The professor's hint about cat owners is supported by data. Confidence: high for shelter data, moderate for vet data.
6. **NYC's shelter is over capacity.** ACC suspended intake in July 2025 for the first time in 30 years, and in July 2026 dog intake still exceeded outflow by 18 dogs. Large dogs wait longest. Confidence: high.
7. **People who love dogs but cannot own one are an untapped resource, and the product already exists.** ACC's BoroughBreaks lets anyone take a shelter dog out for one hour to a full day, and slots fill quickly. Landlord permission and stranger trust block deeper help such as fostering. Confidence: high.
8. **Doormen are a real but invisible node.** Their knowledge legally triggers the Pet Law's 3-month waiver, they hold keys for walkers, and a 2026 Queens case showed key handoffs have no verification. Confidence: moderate to high.
9. **Vet teams are in distress, partly because of owners.** 20% of support staff meet the threshold for serious psychological distress, 98.9% of surveyed vets have been verbally abused by a client, and vets and vet techs carry elevated suicide risk. A project that lowers owner-vet friction creates emotional value on both sides. Confidence: moderate to high.
10. **City capacity and data are thin, so community-built solutions matter.** The Mayor's Office of Animal Welfare had one staff member and under $100,000 in 2025, and about 85,000 dogs are licensed a year against about 530,000 dog-owning households. A focused community project can fill a gap the city cannot, while "an app that does it all" would add one more channel without fixing any single failure above. Confidence: high for the numbers; the conclusion is my judgment.
