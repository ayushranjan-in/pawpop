# What the science says about city dogs and the people who love them

Research date: 2026-10-20. Status: COMPLETE for the midterm. About 28 web searches and 21 page fetches were used; the session's web-search cap was reached at the end, which left the student-homesickness studies unverified (section 8).

How to read this report:
- Every fact carries a source URL and, where the page shows one, the publication date.
- Confidence labels: **high** (peer-reviewed, replicated, or large sample), **moderate** (single study, decent sample, or a named expert's considered view), **low** (small sample, preprint, press coverage only, or mixed evidence).
- "Unverified" marks anything I could not check against a primary source.
- How each study was checked is listed in the source index at the end. I read one paper in full (Duranton and Horowitz, 2019). For the rest I checked the abstract or full-text page directly where the index says "fetched," and otherwise relied on a search engine's summary of the abstract.

**The five findings to lead the midterm with** (details in "Top 10 findings" at the end):
1. Adult dogs depend on their human far more than on other dogs, and many healthy adult dogs do not want dog friends. Veterinary behaviorists call that normal.
2. Small, familiar, well-matched play groups fit the evidence better than dog parks full of strangers. No study has tested "dog friend matching." Playdate-matching apps exist and have mostly failed for lack of users (see research/web/03-competitors-precedents.md), so the science supports the need while the app format has a poor track record.
3. In the Dog Aging Project (21,410 dogs), routine social companionship was linked to health about 5 times more strongly than household money.
4. City dogs are more fearful, and problem behavior is the norm (72.5% of 13,715 dogs). Owners' confusion about "what is normal" is a real need.
5. Dogs make owners walk more and meet more neighbors, but they do not reliably make owners happier or less lonely.

The lab studies the behavior and cognition of New York City pet dogs and recruits dogs and owners from around the city, many of them from the Upper West Side. ### Key study: "Let me sniff!" (Duranton and Horowitz, 2019)
- Citation: Duranton, C., and Horowitz, A. (2019). Let me sniff! Nosework induces positive judgment bias in pet dogs. Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 211, 61-66. Available online 2018-12-06. DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2018.12.009. I read the full paper (6 pages). Charlotte Duranton was at Ethodog, a canine ethology lab near Paris, and the dogs were tested in France, not in New York.
- Design: 20 pet dogs (balanced sex ratio, half of them shepherd breeds) were split into two groups of 10. The nosework group learned to search for a hidden treat with their noses, and owners were told to "follow their dogs, not to guide them." The control group did heelwork (following the owner's steps for a treat). Each group had two group classes and short daily home sessions (about five minutes) for two weeks.
- Measure: a "cognitive bias test", a standard welfare test in which an animal learns that a bowl in one spot holds food and a bowl in another spot is empty, and then meets a bowl in the ambiguous middle spot. A faster approach is read as "optimism", meaning a more positive emotional state.
- Result: the nosework dogs approached the ambiguous bowl faster after training (4.74 seconds before, 3.40 seconds after, p < 0.01, Cohen's d = 0.64, a medium effect). The heelwork dogs did not change significantly (5.28 to 4.40 seconds, p = 0.15).
- The authors' framing, quoted from the paper: "Pet dogs can be considered 'captive' insofar as they cannot choose where to live, where or when to go outside, with whom to interact, or what activity to do and when to do it." They add that pet dogs "are often alone and confined in a limited space during the daytime."
- Their practical recommendation, quoted: owners should "increase their pet dog's foraging time through nosework or any other activity, such as natural sniffing during walks." They also credit autonomy: nosework lets dogs "choose what to do."
- Caveats: only 20 dogs; the paper compares each group to itself rather than formally testing whether the two groups changed by different amounts; the heelwork group also trended faster; and a later study found that dogs can learn that the ambiguous spot is unrewarded when tested twice, which complicates before-and-after designs ("The effect of repeated testing on judgement bias in domestic dogs," https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9465138/, publication date not verified). **Confidence: moderate that sniffing activities help dog welfare; low that this one study proves "optimism" by itself.**

### Key study: toys alone do not make pet dogs measurably happier (Chan, Arellano, and Horowitz, 2023)
- Citation: Chan, K., Arellano, C., and Horowitz, A. (2023). Here Puppy, Chew on This: Short-Term Provision of Toys Does Not Improve Welfare in Companion Dogs. Animals, 13(21), 3340. Published 2023-10-27. https://www.mdpi.com/2076-2615/13/21/3340
- Design: run at the Barnard lab in New York in 2022. Dogs were randomly assigned to 12 days of regular play with study toys matched to the owner's report of the dog's favorite toy type (n = 32) or to a control group (n = 15). The study measured activity, eating speed, and a cognitive bias test.
- Result: toys did not significantly change activity, eating speed, or optimism. Dogs given more complex toys improved slightly on some measures, but not significantly. Most earlier evidence that toys help came from shelter and laboratory dogs, not pet dogs. **Confidence: moderate (one study, small control group, short exposure).** This is directly relevant to the "what owners buy versus what dogs need" question at the end of this report.

### Other lab work relevant to city dogs
- **Elevators (2024).** Student Lila Muscosky (Barnard class of 2024) studied whether dogs in high-rise buildings learn which floor is home. With 29 dogs and 116 elevator rides, dogs could learn to link a landmark (an umbrella stand or a dryer sheet) with their home floor; she called the results somewhat ambiguous. Source: Barnard news, "To Know a Dog, To Love a Dog," 2024-04-29, https://barnard.edu/news/know-dog-love-dog. Confidence: low (student project, not peer-reviewed as far as I found).
- **Shaking off (2024).** Bryce, A., Nurkin, P., and Horowitz, A. (2024). Shake it off: Investigating the function of a domestic dog behavior in social contexts. Animals, 14(22), 3248. Listed on the Barnard profile. I did not read the full text.
- **The dog as a silent partner (2021).** Horowitz, A. (2021). Considering the "Dog" in Dog-Human Interaction. Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 8:642821, published 2021-05-05, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8132963/. This opinion paper argues that research on people and animals usually values the animal only for its effect on the person, and it proposes a vocabulary for the dog's own perspective, agency, and consent. **This is the strongest academic backing for the project's idea of treating the dog as a user who cannot speak.** Confidence: high that she argues this.
- **City dogs versus suburban dogs.** In a 2019 talk, Horowitz reportedly said urban dogs are more social than suburban dogs because they meet other dogs more often. This was an answer to an audience question, not a study result. Source: Williams Record, 2019-11, https://williamsrecord.com/2019/11/alexandra-horowitz-speaks-on-dog-ownership-canine-cognition. Confidence: low (secondhand, informal).
- I found no published Horowitz study that measures how NYC dogs walk (route, sniff time, leash tension). That gap is itself useful: it is an open question the team could raise with the lab.

## 2. Urban dog welfare: behavior problems, noise, fear, leash reactivity, city stressors

**Bottom line:** behavior problems are the norm, not the exception. About three in four pet dogs show at least one problem behavior that owners rate as serious, noise fear is the single most common one, and city living is linked to more fear of other dogs and of strangers. Almost all of this evidence comes from owner surveys, so it shows associations, not causes.

### How common are behavior problems? (Salonen et al., 2020, Finland)
- Citation: Salonen, M., Sulkama, S., Mikkola, S., Puurunen, J., Hakanen, E., Tiira, K., Araujo, C., and Lohi, H. (2020). Prevalence, comorbidity, and breed differences in canine anxiety in 13,700 Finnish pet dogs. Scientific Reports, 10, 2962. Published 2020-03-05. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-59837-z (open access, also PMC7058607). University of Helsinki.
- Sample: 13,715 dogs of 264 breeds, from an online owner questionnaire covering seven anxiety-like traits.
- Findings:
  - 72.5% of dogs had at least one "highly problematic" behavior.
  - **Noise sensitivity** was the most common trait. 32% of dogs were highly fearful of at least one noise, and 26% were afraid of fireworks.
  - **Fear** came second at 29%: fear of other dogs 17%, fear of strangers 15%, fear of new situations 11%.
  - About 20% showed high inattention, 15% high hyperactivity or impulsivity, and 16% compulsive behavior.
  - Traits cluster: fear and aggression often occur together, and noise sensitivity is especially common in fearful dogs.
  - Older dogs had more noise sensitivity, aggression, and fear of surfaces.
- Caveats: owner-reported; Finnish dogs; the "low, moderate, high" categories mostly reflect how often signs occur rather than how severe they are (dvm360 review, https://www.dvm360.com/view/prevalence-comorbidity-and-breed-differences-for-anxious-dogs). **Confidence: high that problem behaviors are very common; moderate on exact percentages for US city dogs.**

### City living is linked to fear of dogs and strangers (Puurunen et al., 2020)
- Citation: Puurunen, J., Hakanen, E., Salonen, M. K., Mikkola, S., Sulkama, S., Araujo, C., and Lohi, H. (2020). Inadequate socialisation, inactivity, and urban living environment are associated with social fearfulness in pet dogs. Scientific Reports, 10, 3527. Published 2020-02-26. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7044223/
- Sample: 5,973 dogs for the fear-of-dogs analysis (19.5% fearful of other dogs) and 5,932 dogs for the fear-of-strangers analysis (15.1% fearful of strangers).
- How "urban" was measured: the researchers mapped land use (built-up surfaces, farmland, forest) within a 3-kilometer radius of each dog's home and combined it into an "urban environment score."
- Findings: dogs living in more urban environments were more likely to fear other dogs and strangers. The strongest risk factor was poor socialisation as a puppy (too little exposure to new people, dogs, and situations). Infrequent training or activities, small body size, female sex, and neutering were also linked to social fear.
- The authors' explanation, quoted: "urban areas, such as cities, may be very hectic and stressful environments to live as they are full of different stimuli, such as sudden and loud noises, potentially predisposing dogs to fear-related and other behavioural problems."
- A follow-up from the same group found that non-social fear (fear of noises, surfaces, and new situations) was also more common in dogs that lived in more urban areas, lived without other dogs, had inexperienced owners, and did fewer activities. Source: Puurunen et al. (2020), Active and social life is associated with lower non-social fearfulness in pet dogs, Scientific Reports 10, 13774, published 2020-08-13, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-70722-7. I did not read this paper in full.
- **Confidence: moderate.** Large sample and real geographic data, but the design is a single-time survey, so a fearful dog could also be the cause of an owner's lifestyle rather than the result of it. Finnish "urban" is less dense than Manhattan.

### Leash reactivity (barking, lunging, or growling at other dogs on a leash)
- **How common:** In a UK owner survey (3,897 completed questionnaires), 22% of owners said their dog currently showed aggression (barking, lunging, growling, or biting) toward unfamiliar dogs, and 8% toward other dogs in the household. Source: Casey, R. A., et al. (2013). Inter-dog aggression in a UK owner survey: prevalence, co-occurrence in different contexts and risk factors. Veterinary Record. https://bvajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/epdf/10.1136/vr.100997. Because barking and lunging count, this number likely includes many "reactive" dogs who would never bite. Confidence: moderate.
- **The leash itself changes behavior:** In an observational study of dogs meeting on walks, threat displays happened about twice as often between leashed dogs as between unleashed dogs (Řezáč, P., et al., 2011, Factors affecting dog-dog interactions on walks with their owners, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168159111002668). A UK study found that a leash on either dog reduced the chance that the two dogs interacted at all (Westgarth, C., et al., 2010, Dog behaviour on walks and the effect of use of the leash, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222991823_Dog_behaviour_on_walks_and_the_effect_of_use_of_the_leash). Note: the search summary I used paired these two titles with the wrong authors; I matched them from my own knowledge of the literature and the 2011 code in the ScienceDirect address, so treat the pairing as moderately confident. Confidence in the findings: moderate.
- **Frustration, not only threat:** A 2026 questionnaire study of "dog reactive dogs" (1,360 complete responses) separated three behavior clusters, "Oral Attack" (snapping, nipping, biting), "Frustration" (barking, lunging, whining), and "Posturing" (growling, stiff posture). About 40% of the dogs showed frustration and posturing but very little biting, which suggests low escalation risk. Source: "Reactive and risky: The behavioural structuring of 'dog reactive dogs'," Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2026, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168159126000523. I read only the search summary, not the paper. Confidence: low to moderate.
- **The owner's side:** A qualitative online survey of owners of reactive dogs found heavy emotional and financial costs (training, equipment, possibly medication), and the lead author called off-leash dogs the most striking problem reactive-dog owners face. Source: "'It's Okay He's Friendly': Understanding the Experience of Owning and Walking a Reactive Dog Using a Qualitative Online Survey," Anthrozoös (DOI registered in 2023; exact publication date not verified; cited in this report as "Anthrozoös, 2024" after the summary's date), https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08927936.2023.2287314; summarized by Companion Animal Psychology, 2024-02, https://www.companionanimalpsychology.com/2024/02/having-reactive-dog-is-difficult-and.html. Confidence: moderate (qualitative, self-selected sample).
- **Why it matters:** An Australian study's background notes that, among owners who euthanized a dog for behavioral reasons, 64.7% named aggression toward other dogs or animals as a factor (cited in "Exploring trainer insights and understanding of on-lead dog aggressive behaviours," 2025, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44338-025-00119-1). Confidence: moderate (secondhand citation).

### City-specific stressors the evidence points to
- Sudden loud noise (traffic, sirens, construction, fireworks): noise fear is the most common problem in Salonen et al. (26% fear fireworks).
- Dense, unavoidable encounters with unfamiliar dogs and people on narrow sidewalks, on a leash that blocks escape (Puurunen; Řezáč).
- Little control over their own day: Duranton and Horowitz (2019) describe pet dogs as "captive" because they cannot choose when to go out, with whom to interact, or what to do (section 1).
- Off-leash dogs approaching leashed dogs, a top complaint of reactive-dog owners (above).
- I found no peer-reviewed study of New York City dogs specifically. **Treat NYC-specific claims as plausible extrapolation, not evidence.**

## 3. Dog social life: do adult dogs need dog friends?

**Bottom line:** adult dogs need people far more reliably than they need dogs. Many dogs enjoy a few familiar dog friends, but a large share of adults become selective after social maturity, and experts say a dog with no dog friends can be perfectly happy. That matches what the team heard in interviews. The evidence favors a small, stable, familiar circle over big groups of strangers.

### Dogs lean on humans more than on other dogs
- **Tuber et al. (1996).** Adult dogs that had lived in pairs with a littermate since about 8 weeks old were put in an unfamiliar room. Their stress hormone (cortisol, measured in blood) rose just as much with their lifelong kennelmate present as when they were alone, but did not rise when their human caretaker was present. The dogs also stayed near and solicited the human more than the other dog. Citation: Tuber, D. S., Sanders, S., Hennessy, M. B., and Miller, J. A. (1996). Behavioral and glucocorticoid responses of adult domestic dogs (Canis familiaris) to companionship and social separation. Journal of Comparative Psychology, 110(1), 103-108. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8851558/. Caveats: kennel-raised laboratory dogs, small sample (I could not verify the exact number), and a 2024 puppy study found that both humans and other dogs can calm puppies (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347224000010). Confidence: moderate.
- **Ferrans, Hare, and McHenry (2026).** 31 adult dogs from multi-dog homes chose between being near their owner or near their own housemate dog. They spent about 39% more time near the owner and wagged their tails more there. When food was the competing option, the owner preference disappeared. Citation: Ferrans, M., Hare, B., and McHenry, J. (2026). Adult companion dogs allocate social proximity toward owners over household dogs under competing contexts. Animal Cognition, 29(1), 43. Published 2026-04-13. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13194234/. Confidence: moderate (small sample, lab arena).

### Social maturity: many adult dogs become choosier
- The American Animal Hospital Association (AAHA) behavior guidelines place social maturity at 12 to 36 months and list fear of, withdrawal from, or aggression toward other dogs as behaviors that can appear then and deserve a veterinary check. They also state there is no evidence that dogs "grow out" of behavior problems. Source: 2015 AAHA Canine and Feline Behavior Management Guidelines, "Age and behavior," https://www.aaha.org/resources/2015-aaha-canine-and-feline-behavior-management-guidelines/age-and-behavior/. Confidence: high that this is mainstream veterinary guidance.
- Trainers and rescue groups use a "sociability scale": dog social, dog tolerant, dog selective, dog aggressive. The American Kennel Club writes that most puppies start out dog social but many become dog tolerant or dog selective as adults (https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/training/understanding-scale-dog-selectivity/, accessed 2026-10-20, date not shown). The rescue group BADRAP describes a dog-selective dog as one that has some dog friends but is choosier and easily offended by rude manners (https://badrap.org/training-resources/dog-dog-tolerance/). This scale is a practitioner tool, not a validated research instrument. Confidence: moderate.

### What named experts say about dogs who do not want dog friends
- Lisa Radosta, DVM, board-certified veterinary behaviorist: "It may seem weird to some, but some dogs are actually really happy not being social with other dogs." And: "The primary obligation is the safety and well-being of your dog."
- Nicholas Dodman, BVMS, board-certified veterinary behaviorist, professor emeritus at Tufts: "For 95 to 98 percent of dogs, the simple answer is yes, they like having doggy buddies. But not every dog wants a doggy buddy and that's okay." His 95-98% figure is an expert estimate, not a measured statistic.
- Both quotes: Arden Moore, "Does Your Dog Need Canine Friends?", Fear Free Happy Homes, https://www.fearfreehappyhomes.com/does-your-dog-need-canine-friends/ (no date shown, accessed 2026-10-20).
- VCA Animal Hospitals (Ellen Lindell, VMD, board-certified veterinary behaviorist, and colleagues; last updated 2025-09-12): "Most adult dogs play best in pairs, so it is best to limit the group to three pairs." On dog parks: "you cannot choose your dog's friends at a public park," and leave if "your own dog repeatedly attempts to retreat or hide." https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/social-groups-for-dogs
- **Confidence: high** that mainstream veterinary behaviorists consider dog-selective or dog-indifferent adults normal.

### Dog parks: fights are rare, but the park raises stress hormones
- **Shyan, Fortune, and King (2003).** In 72 hours of observation at one US dog park over 8 months, researchers saw 177 dogs and 28 conflicts or near-conflicts (under 0.5% of interactions). 14 were clear aggressive episodes, each under a minute, and none caused serious injury. Only 9 dogs (5%) were aggressive. Citation: "Bark Parks" - A Study on Interdog Aggression in a Limited-Control Environment. Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science, 6(1), 25-32. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327604jaws0601_02. Confidence: moderate (one park, old data).
- **Ottenheimer Carrier et al. (2013).** At a dog park in St. John's, Newfoundland, dogs' saliva cortisol rose significantly after 20 minutes in the park (P = 0.013) but not after a 20-minute on-leash walk. Dogs that visited the park less often had higher cortisol (r = -0.34), and a hunched posture went with higher cortisol. Citation: Ottenheimer Carrier, L., Cyr, A., Anderson, R. E., and Walsh, C. J. (2013). Exploring the dog park: Relationships between social behaviours, personality and cortisol in companion dogs. Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 146(1-4), 96-106. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168159113000981. Caveat: cortisol also rises with excitement and fun, so a rise is not proof of distress. Confidence: moderate.
- **Takeaway:** serious fights are uncommon, but the park is an arousing, uncontrolled setting, and newcomers and nervous dogs carry the highest stress load. Dogs at the park cannot choose their company.

### Play: how dogs negotiate it, and does familiarity matter?
- **Play signals.** Marc Bekoff (University of Colorado) showed that the play bow (front end down, rear end up) clusters around actions that could be misread as real aggression, such as biting with head-shaking, which helps keep play going (Bekoff, M., 1995, Play signals as punctuation: the structure of social play in canids, Behaviour, 132(5), 419-429, https://www.wellbeingintlstudiesrepository.org/acwp_ena/30/). Later work on adult dogs found bows mostly restart play after a pause (Byosiere, Espinosa, and Smuts, 2016, Behavioural Processes, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376635716300286). Confidence: high that play is actively negotiated with signals.
- **Attention in play.** Horowitz (2009) videotaped pairs of dogs playing (naturalistic observation) and found dogs get their partner's attention before signaling play, using stronger attention-getters (nips, paws, bumps) when the partner is distracted. Citation: Horowitz, A. (2009). Attention to attention in domestic dog (Canis familiaris) dyadic play. Animal Cognition, 12, 107-118. https://psychology.barnard.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Attention%20to%20Attention.pdf. Confidence: high.
- **Play is not 50:50.** Adult dog pairs do not split "winning" moves evenly; older and more dominant dogs win more and hold back less (Bauer and Smuts, 2007, Animal Behaviour, as summarized in https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4864279/). In 49 dogs observed at an off-leash dog park, more lopsided play meant shorter play sessions (Cordoni, G., Nicotra, V., and Palagi, E., 2016, Unveiling the "secret" of play in dogs: Asymmetry and signals, Journal of Comparative Psychology, https://www.istc.cnr.it/en/content/unveiling-secret-play-dogs-canis-lupus-familiaris-asymmetry-and-signals). This is the closest research support for "play-style compatibility": mismatched, one-sided play tends to end quickly. Confidence: moderate.
- **Familiar versus unfamiliar dogs.** In 15-minute off-leash sessions with kennel-housed dogs, unfamiliar pairs had more contact in the first 3 minutes, but by minutes 9 to 12 familiar pairs stayed closer together while unfamiliar pairs drifted apart (Pullen, A. J., Merrill, R. J. N., and Bradshaw, J. W. S., 2013, The effect of familiarity on behavior of kenneled dogs during interactions with conspecifics, Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science, 16(1), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23282294/). Cordoni et al. (2016), above, found play asymmetry did not differ between "friends" and "strangers." Evidence on familiarity is thin and mixed. Confidence: low to moderate.
- I found **no study that tests "play-style matching" as a way to pick dog friends.** The team's "small stable friend group" idea is consistent with the evidence above, but nobody has tested it directly.

## 4. The Dog Aging Project: social determinants of dog health

**What it is:** the Dog Aging Project is a long-term US study of tens of thousands of companion dogs, supported by the National Institute on Aging, that tracks how genes, lifestyle, and environment affect healthy aging. Owners enroll their own dogs and report on them through detailed surveys, such as the Health and Life Experience Survey used in the papers below. Overview: https://www.nia.nih.gov/news/dog-aging-project-goal-help-both-dogs-and-humans-live-longer-healthier-lives.

### Social companionship mattered about five times more than money (McCoy et al., 2023)
- Citation: McCoy, B. M., Brassington, L., Jin, K., Dolby, G. A., Shrager, S., Collins, D., Dunbar, M., Dog Aging Project Consortium, Ruple, A., and Snyder-Mackler, N. (2023). Social determinants of health and disease in companion dogs: a cohort study from the Dog Aging Project. Evolution, Medicine, and Public Health, 11(1), 187-201. Published 2023-05-13. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10306367/
- Sample: 21,410 dogs, owner survey from 2019-2020.
- The researchers found five factors describing a dog's social environment: neighborhood stability, household income, time with children, time with other animals, and owner age. "Social time" was measured as routine hours per day spent with dogs, children, and adults.
- Findings, quoted: "The effect of social support was 5× stronger than financial factors." Financial and household adversity went with poorer health and mobility; social companionship, especially living with other animals, went with better health. The paper also states that "a more urban environment with less stability was associated with worse health."
- Limitations stated by the authors: all data are owner-reported; the design cannot establish cause ("we were not able to determine causality"); and the sample is wealthy, with a median household income of $100,000 to $119,999 against a US median of $67,521.
- **Confidence: moderate.** Huge sample, but owner-reported health and correlation only. A healthier, more sociable dog may also be the kind that ends up in a multi-dog home.
- **Project implication:** the strongest social-health signal in dogs comes from routine companionship (daily time with other animals and people), not from money spent. This supports designing for regular, repeated time together rather than products.

### Neighborhood disadvantage and dog walking (Collins et al., 2022)
- Citation: Collins, D., Lee, H., Dunbar, M. D., Crowder, K., and Dog Aging Project Consortium (2022). Associations between Neighborhood Disadvantage and Dog Walking among Participants in the Dog Aging Project. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 19(18), 11179. Published 2022-09-06. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9517596/
- Sample: 19,732 owners. Finding, quoted: "each standard deviation increase in the disadvantage index is associated with approximately 55 fewer walking minutes per week in the full model." (A search summary claimed 79 minutes. The paper's full model says about 55, so I use 55.)
- Confidence: moderate (correlation; on-leash walking only).

### Activity and dog dementia (Yarborough et al., 2022)
- Citation: Yarborough, S., Fitzpatrick, A., Schwartz, S. M., et al. (2022). Evaluation of cognitive function in the Dog Aging Project: associations with baseline canine characteristics. Scientific Reports, 12, 13316. Published 2022-08-25. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9411588/
- Sample: 15,019 dogs. 1.4% met the criteria for canine cognitive dysfunction (a dementia-like condition). The odds rose 52% with each year of age. Dogs reported as not active had 6.47 times the odds of cognitive dysfunction compared with very active dogs (95% confidence interval 2.93 to 17.23).
- The authors warn about reverse causation, quoted: the link "could also exist simply because of dogs exhibiting less activity due to their cognitive decline." Confidence: moderate for the association, low for causation.

### City versus rural dogs in the Dog Aging Project
- I found no Dog Aging Project paper that directly compares the health of urban and rural dogs. The closest findings are the "more urban, less stable environment" result in McCoy et al. above and a 2025 regional comparison of 47,444 dogs (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12324818/), which I did not read in full. The project has linked home and neighborhood environmental data for over 40,000 dogs (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12369580/), so urban-rural results may appear later. **Confidence: unknown for any direct city-versus-country health claim.**

## 5. Human benefits and changes

**Bottom line:** the strongest, most consistent human effects of dogs are practical and social: more walking and more contact with neighbors and strangers. The popular claim that pets cure loneliness and improve mental health is much weaker than media coverage suggests (Herzog, 2011), and the research is mixed. Identity change ("pet parent") and grief are real and documented, mostly through qualitative studies.

### Walking and physical activity (strong)
- Citation: Westgarth, C., Christley, R. M., Jewell, C., German, A. J., Boddy, L. M., and Christian, H. E. (2019). Dog owners are more likely to meet physical activity guidelines than people without a dog. Scientific Reports. DOI 10.1038/s41598-019-41254-6. Published 2019-04-18. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6473089/
- Sample: 191 dog-owning adults, 455 adults without dogs, and 46 children in 385 households in West Cheshire, UK (2015). Survey answers were checked against activity trackers for 28 adults.
- Findings: dog owners had about four times the odds of meeting the 150-minutes-a-week activity guideline (odds ratio 4.10, 95% CI 2.05 to 8.19). Owners averaged 9.6 walks and 347 minutes of walking a week, versus 4.6 walks and 159 minutes for non-owners. Dog walking added to other exercise rather than replacing it.
- Caveat: "four times the odds" is not "four times as likely," because most people in both groups met the guideline. The data are mostly self-reported and cross-sectional. **Confidence: high that dog owners walk more; moderate on the size of the effect.**

### Dogs as social catalysts (strong and robust)
- Citation: McNicholas, J., and Collis, G. M. (2000). Dogs as catalysts for social interactions: Robustness of the effect. British Journal of Psychology, 91(1), 61-70. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10717771/
- Design: University of Warwick researchers used a highly trained dog that did not seek attention from passers-by, across normal daily activities, not just dog-walking areas. In the second study the handler dressed either smartly or scruffily.
- Findings: having the dog present increased social interactions, especially with strangers. Smart clothes helped a little, but the dog's presence was the largest effect regardless of how the handler looked. The authors concluded the effect is "very robust." **Confidence: high.**

### Pets and neighborhood social capital: "The Pet Factor" (Wood et al., 2015)
- Citation: Wood, L., Martin, K., Christian, H., Nathan, A., Lauritsen, C., Houghton, S., Kawachi, I., and McCune, S. (2015). The Pet Factor: Companion Animals as a Conduit for Getting to Know People, Friendship Formation and Social Support. PLOS ONE, 10(4), e0122085. Published 2015-04-29. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0122085
- Sample: 2,692 randomly selected adults surveyed by phone in Perth (Australia), San Diego, Portland, and Nashville.
- Findings:
  - Pet owners were more likely than non-owners to get to know people in their neighborhood (odds ratio 1.61).
  - In the three US cities, dog owners were more likely than other pet owners to count people they met through their pet as friends (odds ratio 2.59).
  - About 40% of pet owners received at least one kind of support (emotional, informational, appraisal, or practical help) from people they met through their pet.
  - About half of pet owners had got to know someone through their pet.
- "Social capital" here means the trust, ties, and mutual help that exist among neighbors.
- Caveat: a single-time survey cannot show that pets cause friendships; sociable people may also be more likely to own and walk dogs. **Confidence: moderate.**
- **Project implication:** dog-mediated human ties are a real, measured phenomenon, and most of them begin as weak ties (recognizing and chatting with neighbors) that sometimes become friendships and support. The team's "small stable dog friend group" also creates a small stable human friend group.

### Loneliness: the evidence is mixed, sometimes null
- **Gilbey and Tani (2015)**, a systematic review (a structured summary of all qualifying studies) of quantitative studies, concluded, quoted: "Overall, we judged none of the included studies as providing convincing evidence that companion animals help to alleviate loneliness." Citation: Gilbey, A., and Tani, K. (2015). Companion Animals and Loneliness: A Systematic Review of Quantitative Studies. Anthrozoös, 28(2), 181-197. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273063714_Companion_Animals_and_Loneliness_A_Systematic_Review_of_Quantitative_Studies
- **Kretzler, König, and Hajek (2022)** reviewed 24 observational studies. Pet ownership was linked to less social isolation in adults. For loneliness, studies before COVID-19 showed no overall significant link, while studies during the pandemic mostly found pets helped. Studies of children and adolescents suggested a protective link. Most studies found no difference between dogs, cats, and other pets. Citation: Kretzler, B., König, H.-H., and Hajek, A. (2022). Pet ownership, loneliness, and social isolation: a systematic review. Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology, 57(10), 1935-1957. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9272860/
- **Confidence: high that the evidence is mixed.** The defensible claim is "dogs create opportunities for contact, and some people use them," not "dogs cure loneliness." A lonely owner whose dog does not fit the neighborhood's dog scene (for example a reactive dog) may get the cost without the social benefit.

### Mental health and well-being: the "pet effect" is not established
- **Herzog (2011).** Psychologist Harold Herzog (Western Carolina University) reviewed the field and concluded that a general "pet effect" on mental and physical health is "at present not a fact but an unsubstantiated hypothesis." Some studies find benefits, others find pet owners no healthier or happier, and some find them worse off. Citation: Herzog, H. (2011). The Impact of Pets on Human Health and Psychological Well-Being: Fact, Fiction, or Hypothesis? Current Directions in Psychological Science, 20(4), 236-239. Published online 2011-08-08. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0963721411415220. Confidence: high that the field is contested.
- **Parsons et al. (2024), UK.** 6,018 UK adults were followed for 12 months from April 2020. Pet ownership was not associated with better depression, anxiety, anhedonia (loss of pleasure), or loneliness scores at the start or over time; owners had slightly worse symptoms on average. Dog owners were a bit more likely to exercise daily or almost daily (40% versus 35% of people with no pets). Among people living alone, cat and dog owners were slightly less lonely, but the effects were small. Citation: Parsons, C. E., Landberger, C., Purves, K. L., and Young, K. S. (2024). No beneficial associations between living with a pet and mental health outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic in a large UK longitudinal sample. Mental Health and Prevention, 35, 200354. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2212657024000369; press release 2024-09, https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1056745. Confidence: moderate (pandemic period).
- **Mokos, Kubinyi, et al. (2025), Hungary.** In a random, population-representative sample of 2,783 adults surveyed three times during the pandemic, the 65 people who got a pet had a short boost in cheerfulness lasting 1 to 4 months. Over up to 6 months, getting a pet, especially a dog, was linked to declines in calmness, activity, cheerfulness, and life satisfaction. The 75 people who lost a pet showed no significant change in well-being. The authors suggest the demands of dog care can outweigh the early lift. Citation: Mokos, J., Kubinyi, E., et al. (2025). Short-term effects of pet acquisition and loss on well-being in an unbiased sample during the COVID-19 pandemic. Scientific Reports, 15, 20267. Published 2025-07-01. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12215059/; summary at https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/07/250701234744.htm. Confidence: low to moderate (small numbers of new and bereaved owners, pandemic conditions).
- **Project implication:** a dog does not automatically make a person happier. The burden of care (time, money, behavior problems) can cancel the benefit. Designs that lower the care burden and raise the quality of the relationship are aiming at the real lever.

### Identity: becoming a "pet parent"
- **Owens and Grauerholz (2019)**, a qualitative sociology study of people who count their dogs and cats as family, found that not everyone who calls a pet "family" sees themselves as a "parent." People with young children stressed how pets differ from kids, while childfree people and people with grown children stressed the similarities. Citation: Owens, N., and Grauerholz, L. (2019). Interspecies Parenting: How Pet Parents Construct Their Roles. Humanity and Society, 43(2), 96-119 (online 2018-01-02). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0160597617748166. Confidence: moderate (qualitative, small sample).
- **Laurent-Simpson (2021)**, sociologist at Southern Methodist University, describes the "multispecies family," including "pet parents," "animal kids," and grandparents with "granddogs," using identity theory (the idea that people build identity from social roles). Her data on childfree households suggest dogs and cats can fill a child-like role. Citation: Laurent-Simpson, A. (2021). Just Like Family: How Companion Animals Joined the Household. NYU Press. https://nyupress.org/9781479852628/just-like-family/; press summary at https://phys.org/news/2021-07-sociologist-pet-parents-pets-family.html (2021-07). Confidence: moderate.
- Counterpoint: sociologist Leslie Irvine (University of Colorado Boulder) has argued that a pet's "child" role is not the same as a human child's (as reported in coverage of this literature; I did not find the primary quote). Confidence: low.

### Pet-loss grief and "disenfranchised grief"
- "Disenfranchised grief" means grief that other people do not acknowledge or treat as legitimate, so the mourner feels unable to express it openly. Spain et al. (below) define it this way. The term comes from death-and-grief researcher Kenneth Doka's 1989 book Disenfranchised Grief: Recognizing Hidden Sorrow (from my own knowledge; not re-verified in this session).
- **Spain, O'Dwyer, and Moston (2019)** surveyed 133 Australian adults who had lost a pet. When grief is treated as illegitimate, bereaved owners can face more distress and lower quality of life. Memorials were more common when grief was severe and when it was disenfranchised, which suggests people use them to cope and to make their grief feel legitimate, though memorials were not linked to "posttraumatic growth" (positive personal change after loss). Citation: Spain, B., O'Dwyer, L., and Moston, S. (2019). Pet Loss: Understanding Disenfranchised Grief, Memorial Use, and Posttraumatic Growth. Anthrozoös, 32(4), 555-568. Published online 2019-06-28. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334092302_Pet_Loss_Understanding_Disenfranchised_Grief_Memorial_Use_and_Posttraumatic_Growth. Confidence: moderate.
- **Project implication:** the end of a dog's life is a design moment, and the grief is real but socially unsupported. A dog friend group that also mourns together would address a documented gap.

## 6. Dog communication: body language, talking buttons, AI bark analysis

**Bottom line:** owners notice loud distress (trembling, whining, growling) but routinely miss the quiet early signals (looking away, lip licking, yawning, moving away). Most bites to young children come from familiar dogs at home, during interactions the child starts and adults tend to read as friendly. Talking buttons have some real but narrow evidence behind them. AI "bark translation" is early research that recognizes the situation a bark occurs in, not the dog's feelings.

### What owners miss in dog body language
- **Mariti et al. (2012).** In 1,190 owner questionnaires in Italy, owners most often named trembling and whining as stress signs, then aggression, excessive barking, and panting. Subtle early signs (looking away, turning the head, yawning, nose licking) were named much less often. The authors conclude that few owners can spot stress early enough to step in. Citation: Mariti, C., Gazzano, A., Moore, J. L., Baragli, P., Chelli, L., and Sighieri, C. (2012). Perception of dogs' stress by their owners. Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 7(4), 213-219. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1558787811001547. Caveat: it measures which signs owners name, not how well they read real dogs. Confidence: moderate.
- **Demirbas et al. (2016).** Adults watched three videos of small children with dogs that an expert panel had rated as fearful or anxious. Most of the 71 participants judged the dogs relaxed (68.4%) and confident (65.1%), and the most common labels were "play" and "friendly." People without dogs read the dogs better than dog owners did. The dogs showed only subtle signs (lip licking, looking away, moving away), not growls. Citation: Demirbas, Y. S., Ozturk, H., Emre, B., Kockaya, M., Ozvardar, T., and Scott, A. (2016). Adults' Ability to Interpret Canine Body Language during a Dog-Child Interaction. Anthrozoös, 29(4), 581-596. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/08927936.2016.1228750. Summary: Companion Animal Psychology, 2017-05, https://www.companionanimalpsychology.com/2017/05/people-mistakenly-think-anxious-dogs.html. Caveat: small sample from Turkey. Confidence: moderate.

### Bite risk to children comes mostly from familiar dogs at home
- **Reisner et al. (2011).** Of 203 children treated for dog bites at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, 72% knew the dog. Most bites to children under 7 happened indoors, during positive interactions the child started, with a stationary, familiar dog, and 70% of those bites were to the face. Older children were mostly bitten outdoors by unfamiliar dogs. Citation: Reisner, I. R., Nance, M. L., Zeller, J. S., Houseknecht, E. M., Kassam-Adams, N., and Wiebe, D. J. (2011). Behavioural characteristics associated with dog bites to children presenting to an urban trauma centre. Injury Prevention, 17(5), 348-353. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21444335/. Confidence: high.
- **Reisner et al. (2007).** Among 103 dogs referred to a university behavior clinic after biting a child, resource guarding (protecting food, toys, or places) was the most common trigger (61%). Food guarding was the most common context for bites to familiar children (42%). Two-thirds of the dogs had never bitten a child before, and three-quarters showed anxiety. Citation: Reisner, I. R., Shofer, F. S., and Nance, M. L. (2007). Behavioral assessment of child-directed canine aggression. Injury Prevention, 13, 348-351. https://europepmc.org/api/getPdf?pmcid=PMC2610618. Caveat: clinic sample of dogs already referred for aggression. Confidence: moderate.
- **Project implication:** "My dog is friendly" is exactly the belief the evidence warns about. A tool that teaches owners to see the quiet signals, or that lets the dog "say no" before it has to growl, targets a measured gap.

### Talking buttons (FluentPet, "Bunny the dog") and the UC San Diego research
- **Context:** soundboard buttons play a recorded word when a pet presses them. The trend went viral through social media dogs such as Bunny, whose owner Alexis Devine posts her button use (follower counts and the famous "ouch, paw" foxtail episode are in the citizen-science subsection below). FluentPet, founded in 2020, sells the buttons.
- **Bastos et al. (2024), UC San Diego Comparative Cognition Lab (Federico Rossano, senior author).** 59 button-trained dogs were tested at home (30 by researchers, 29 by owners following a protocol). Researchers could not hear or see which word they played, to rule out unconscious cues. Dogs showed play behavior after "play" words and outside-oriented behavior after "outside" words, about seven times more than otherwise, whether the word came from the owner or a stranger, spoken or played from a button. Food words showed no clear effect. Citation: Bastos, A. P. M., et al. (2024). How do soundboard-trained dogs respond to human button presses? An investigation into word comprehension. PLOS ONE, 19(8), e0307189. Published 2024-08-28. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0307189
- **Conflict of interest:** the paper discloses that six authors have consulted for FluentPet and two are FluentPet employees.
- **Follow-up:** a December 2024 Scientific Reports paper analyzing button-press logs found dogs' two-button combinations were not random and not simple copies of their owners' presses. Source: Soundboard-trained dogs produce non-accidental, non-random and non-imitative two-button combinations, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-79517-6. I did not read it in full.
- **What this does and does not show:** it shows dogs can link certain recorded words to events (comprehension). It does not show that dogs "talk" in sentences or express abstract thoughts. **Confidence: moderate for word comprehension; low for any claim of dog language.**

### AI bark and emotion analysis
- **Abzaliev, Pérez-Espinosa, and Mihalcea (2024), University of Michigan with Mexico's INAOE institute.** They adapted Wav2Vec2, an AI model pre-trained on human speech, to recordings of 74 dogs (mostly Chihuahuas, poodles, and schnauzers in Mexico). The human-speech pre-training helped: identifying individual dogs rose from 23.7% (trained on dog data only) to 49.95%, and breed reached about 62%. The model could tell very aggressive barking at a stranger from normal barking. Citation: Towards Dog Bark Decoding: Leveraging Human Speech Processing for Automated Bark Classification. LREC-COLING 2024, pages 16480-16486. https://aclanthology.org/2024.lrec-main.1432/; press release 2024-06-04, https://news.umich.edu/using-ai-to-decode-dog-vocalizations/
- **Caveat:** the "70% accuracy at reading emotion" headlines overstate it. The model classifies the situation in which a bark was recorded, not a measured emotion, and gender classification barely beat guessing. **Confidence: high that bark "translation" for consumers is not scientifically established as of 2026.**
- **Other work I found but did not read:** a 2025 Interspeech paper ("Dog2vec") pre-trains a model on dog sounds instead of human speech (https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/li25o_interspeech.pdf), and a 2025 Scientific Reports paper tests whether large vision-language AI models can recognize dog emotions from images ("Investigating the capabilities of large vision language models in dog emotion recognition," https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-25199-7). Treat both as signs of an active research field, not as evidence of working products.

### The largest button study so far (citizen science)
- A UC San Diego article (written by the university, so not independent) reports that Rossano's citizen-science project now covers about 10,000 dogs and 700 cats in 47 countries. The median dog uses 9 buttons, and 526 dogs had produced multi-button sequences as of late 2024. Rossano notes that not all dogs use buttons, most use only a few, and expressions of emotion are rare compared with requests for basic needs. Bunny's owner is Alexis Devine; the article credits Bunny with 8.5 million TikTok followers and 2 million Instagram followers, and retells how Bunny pressed "mad," "ouch," "stranger," and "paw" in sequence when a foxtail (a barbed grass seed) was stuck in her paw. That story is an anecdote, not data. Source: Robyn Schelenz, UC San Diego, "Can our pets really say 'I love you'? Science is finding out," Phys.org, 2026-02-13, https://phys.org/news/2026-02-pets-science.html. Confidence: moderate for the counts, low for interpretation.

## 7. Enrichment for city dogs: sniffing, decompression walks, nosework

**Bottom line:** the science supports letting dogs use their noses and make choices, but almost nothing has tested the specific popular formats ("decompression walks," "sniffaris") directly. The evidence base is small and the claims online run ahead of it.

### Terms in plain words
- **Nosework (scent work):** structured games where a dog searches for a hidden treat or odor.
- **Sniffari (sniff plus safari):** a walk where the dog chooses the route and sniffs as long as it likes.
- **Decompression walk:** a calm walk in a quiet natural place, usually on a long line (a leash much longer than the standard 6 feet), where the dog moves freely, sniffs, and meets few triggers. It is a trainer term; I could not verify who coined it.

### What the evidence shows
- **Nosework improved an "optimism" measure** in 20 pet dogs over two weeks (Duranton and Horowitz, 2019, section 1). Confidence: moderate.
- **Scoping review (2024).** A University of Adelaide team reviewed 27 studies with 772 shelter, working, and companion dogs and found that little is known about how scent activities affect dogs' behavior and physiology or whether they benefit the dogs themselves. Only four studies measured heart rate. Source: "The Value of Sniffing: A Scoping Review of Scent Activities for Canines," December 2024, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387385399_The_Value_of_Sniffing_A_Scoping_Review_of_Scent_Activities_for_Canines. I did not confirm the journal. Confidence: high that the evidence base is thin.
- **Heart rate studies are mixed.** A 2025 study of 29 dogs found one heart-rate-variability measure was lower during food searching than during spontaneous sniffing, which the authors read as higher arousal when searching (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159125003983). A 2026 study of 21 dogs found heart rate peaked during goal-directed searching and fell afterward at similar rates as after a control task, with tails carried higher while searching (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159126000778). Structured searching seems to be exciting rather than calming. I read only summaries of both. Confidence: low.
- **Leash length and sniffing (not peer-reviewed).** French trainers Cristina and Aurélien Budzinski timed 61 dogs walking 5 minutes each on a 5-foot leash, a 16-foot leash, and off leash. Dogs sniffed an average of 37 seconds on the short leash, 103 seconds on the long leash, and 119 seconds off leash, and their pulse dropped while sniffing. This self-published study ("At the Heart of the Walk," dogfieldstudy.com) is the usual source for the claim that sniffing lowers a dog's heart rate. **Confidence: low.** It is still a vivid and plausible data point for a design concept: a short city leash cuts sniffing roughly to a third.
- **Toys alone did not help pet dogs** in the 2023 Barnard study (section 1). Confidence: moderate.
- **Trainers believe in it.** A 2025 survey of 566 dog trainers found 85% use sniffaris and believe scent activities reduce fear, anxiety, and overexcitement ("Scent work is incorporated in almost everything I do," Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2025, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168159125003351). This measures belief, not effect.
- **Outings are not automatically calming.** Shelter dogs taken on 2.5-hour community outings had higher cortisol that afternoon, back to normal the next day (Gunter, L. M., et al., 2021, Investigating the Impact of Brief Outings on the Welfare of Dogs Living in US Shelters, Animals, 11(2), 548, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7923296/). Confidence: moderate.

### Practical reading for a city
- What the evidence supports: more time with the nose down, more choice about where to go and when to stop, fewer forced encounters, and a longer leash where it is safe and legal. These are low-cost and low-risk.
- What it does not yet support: specific claims such as "20 minutes of sniffing equals an hour of walking" or "sniffing lowers cortisol." I found no study behind either claim.
- NYC constraint: leashes in public may be no longer than 6 feet. The team's companion reports document this rule (research/web/05-stakeholders-ecosystem.md cites NYC311, "Unleashed Dog," undated, checked 2026-10-20, https://portal.311.nyc.gov/article/?kanumber=KA-01051; research/web/01-nyc-dog-life.md cites the city's 2025 housing guide). I did not fetch these pages myself. If a 6-foot leash is the norm, a long-line decompression walk is only possible in designated off-leash areas or private spaces, which makes free sniffing time a scarce resource for New York dogs.

## 8. Students and dogs

**Bottom line:** a short session of hands-on petting measurably lowers students' stress hormone right afterward, and touching matters more than watching. I could not verify any study that measures how much students miss dogs left at home, so that question is open and worth asking in interviews.

### Therapy-dog sessions reduce student stress (Pendry and Vandagriff, 2019)
- Citation: Pendry, P., and Vandagriff, J. L. (2019). Animal Visitation Program (AVP) Reduces Cortisol Levels of University Students: A Randomized Controlled Trial. AERA Open. Published 2019-04-01. DOI https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858419852592 (abstract verified through the Semantic Scholar record, https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1177/2332858419852592).
- Design: 249 undergraduates were randomly assigned to one of four 10-minute conditions: hands-on petting of cats and dogs (n = 73), watching others pet the animals (n = 62), a slideshow of the same animals (n = 57), or a waitlist (n = 57). Students gave saliva samples on waking and 15 and 25 minutes after the session.
- Result: the hands-on group had lower cortisol afterward than the slideshow (beta = .150, p = .046), waitlist (beta = .152, p = .033), and watching groups (beta = .164, p = .040). The authors conclude, quoted: "A 10-minute college-based AVP providing hands-on petting of cats and dogs provides momentary stress relief."
- Caveats: the effect is small and short-term ("momentary"), and cats and dogs were mixed. **Confidence: high that hands-on contact gives short-term relief; low for any lasting effect.**
- **Project implication:** physical contact, not just seeing animals online, drives the effect. A Columbia version of "borrow a dog friend" would need real, repeated, hands-on time.

### Homesickness and missing pets
- **Binfet and Passmore (2016)** studied a therapy-dog program for homesick first-year university students. Title, authors, and date are verified: "Hounds and Homesickness: The Effects of an Animal-assisted Therapeutic Intervention for First-Year University Students," Anthrozoös, published 2016-08-17, DOI https://doi.org/10.1080/08927936.2016.1181364. The abstract was not accessible to me, so **its results are unverified here.**
- **Adamle, Riley, and Carlson (2009)** surveyed college students' interest in pet therapy: "Evaluating College Student Interest in Pet Therapy," Journal of American College Health, published 2009-03-01, DOI https://doi.org/10.3200/JACH.57.5.545-548. **Its findings are unverified here** for the same reason.
- I found no verified number for how many students have a dog at home or how strongly they miss it. **Confidence: unknown.** The team can measure this directly with a two-question survey of classmates.
- Related local fact: the Barnard Dog Cognition Lab runs student research projects with NYC dogs (section 1), which is a ready-made route for students to spend structured time with dogs.

## Gaps: what nobody has studied (useful for the "whimsical solution" half of the project)
- No study tests matching dogs into small, stable friend groups by play style or temperament. The pieces exist (play asymmetry, familiarity, social maturity), but nobody has run the intervention. This is a research gap, not a market gap: playdate-matching apps have been tried many times (research/web/03-competitors-precedents.md).
- No peer-reviewed study of New York City dogs' daily welfare, walking patterns, or sniff time. The Barnard lab is the obvious partner for this question.
- No peer-reviewed test of "decompression walks" or "sniffaris" as such.
- No validated product that translates barks or reads a dog's emotions for owners.
- No verified data on students who miss dogs left at home.

| **Choice and control over the walk.** The authors call pet dogs "captive" because they cannot choose when to go out, with whom to interact, or what to do (Duranton and Horowitz, 2019). Strength: moderate (expert framing). | Owner-paced walks on a short leash; scheduled walker visits. | A self-published trainer study found dogs sniffed 37 seconds on a 5-foot leash versus 103 seconds on a 16-foot leash in 5 minutes (low confidence). NYC's 6-foot leash rule limits this. |
| **Their human.** A human caretaker, not a lifelong dog companion, buffered stress in a new place (Tuber et al., 1996); adult dogs chose owners over housemate dogs (Ferrans et al., 2026). Strength: moderate. | Cameras, treat dispensers, and apps for when the owner is away. | I found no evidence that remote gadgets replace presence (none found, not "proven useless"). |
| **Routine social companionship.** Daily time with other animals and people was linked to better health, about 5 times the effect of household income (McCoy et al., 2023). Strength: moderate (correlation). | Occasional dog-park trips; daycare. | The park raised cortisol after 20 minutes while an on-leash walk did not (Ottenheimer Carrier et al., 2013), and owners cannot choose who is there (VCA, 2025). |
| **A few compatible, familiar dog friends, only if the dog wants them.** Most adult dogs play best in pairs (VCA, 2025); familiar pairs stay together longer (Pullen et al., 2013); lopsided play ends sooner (Cordoni et al., 2016). Strength: low to moderate. | Group play with whoever shows up. | Many adult dogs become dog-selective at 1 to 3 years (AAHA, 2015; AKC). Playdate-matching apps have mostly failed for lack of users (competitors report), and no study has tested matching. |
| **Calm, predictable exposure and early socialization.** Poor puppy socialization was the strongest risk factor for social fear; urban living and inactivity also mattered (Puurunen et al., 2020). Strength: moderate. | Puppy classes for some dogs; after that, mostly unplanned exposure on streets and in parks (my characterization, not measured). | Fear of other dogs (17% to 19.5%) and of strangers (15%) is common in adult dogs (Salonen; Puurunen). |
| **Exercise.** Inactive dogs had 6.47 times the odds of dementia-like decline (Yarborough et al., 2022; reverse causation possible). Strength: moderate. | Walks (aligned with the need). | Owners in poorer neighborhoods walked about 55 fewer minutes a week per step of disadvantage (Collins et al., 2022). |
| **An owner who can read them.** Owners miss the quiet stress signals (Mariti, 2012; Demirbas, 2016), and most bites to young children come from familiar dogs during friendly contact (Reisner, 2011). Strength: moderate to high. | Talking buttons; AI "bark translator" apps. | Buttons show narrow word comprehension (Bastos et al., 2024); bark AI classifies situations, not feelings (Abzaliev et al., 2024). The proven gap is human literacy in dog body language. |

**Summary:** the evidence points to time, choice, sniffing, the owner's presence, and a few well-chosen companions. None of these is an object an owner can buy, which is the core tension for any product idea.

## How owning or caring for a dog changes a person (emotional, social, practical, identity), with evidence

| Dimension | What changes | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Emotional** | Short bursts of calm from touch. | 10 minutes of petting lowered students' cortisol versus watching, slideshow, or waitlist (Pendry and Vandagriff, 2019, n = 249, randomized). | High for short-term relief |
| Emotional | Deep attachment, and grief when the dog dies, often unsupported by others. | Disenfranchised grief and memorial use in 133 bereaved owners (Spain et al., 2019). | Moderate |
| Emotional | No reliable lasting happiness boost; care can be a burden. | "Pet effect" is "an unsubstantiated hypothesis" (Herzog, 2011); no mental-health benefit in 6,018 UK adults (Parsons et al., 2024); new dog owners declined in calmness and life satisfaction over 6 months (Mokos et al., 2025). Reactive-dog owners report heavy emotional and financial cost (Anthrozoös, 2024). | Moderate (mixed) |
| **Social** | More conversations with strangers. | A trained dog increased interactions, especially with strangers, regardless of the handler's clothes (McNicholas and Collis, 2000). | High |
| Social | More neighborhood acquaintances, some friendships and support. | Pet owners had 1.61 times the odds of getting to know neighbors; US dog owners had 2.59 times the odds of making friends through the pet; about 40% got some support from pet-met people (Wood et al., 2015, n = 2,692). | Moderate |
| Social | Loneliness does not reliably fall. | Gilbey and Tani (2015) found no convincing evidence; Kretzler et al. (2022) found mixed results. | High that it is mixed |
| **Practical** | More walking and outdoor time. | 347 versus 159 walking minutes a week; 4.10 times the odds of meeting activity guidelines (Westgarth et al., 2019). | High |
| Practical | A daily routine built around the dog, plus new costs and constraints. | Dog owners were more likely to exercise daily (40% versus 35%) but not more likely to keep a daily schedule (Parsons et al., 2024). | Moderate |
| Practical | New safety duties (supervising children, managing encounters). | Bites to young children happen mostly with familiar dogs at home (Reisner et al., 2011). | High |
| **Identity** | "Pet parent" and multispecies-family identity. | Qualitative work shows people construct parent-like roles, shaped by whether they have human children (Owens and Grauerholz, 2019; Laurent-Simpson, 2021). | Moderate |
| Identity | A self-image tied to the dog's behavior ("good dog, good owner"). | Reactive-dog owners describe difficult emotions and real costs on walks (Anthrozoös, 2024). The identity link is my inference, not a finding of that study. | Low |

**The person-change story the evidence supports:** a new owner walks more, meets more neighbors, and takes on a parent-like role and new duties. The emotional payoff depends on how well the dog fits the owner's life. When the dog is fearful or reactive, the same daily walk becomes a source of stress and cost rather than connection. The design opportunity is to move owners from the second path to the first.

## Which of the 15 core meanings from Making Meaning show up in the evidence

**Source of the list:** Nathan Shedroff, "Meaning Model and Strategy," https://www.nathan.com/tool-meaning-model-strategy/ (undated, accessed 2026-10-20), which presents the 15 core meanings from Diller, S., Shedroff, N., and Rhea, D., Making Meaning: How Successful Businesses Deliver Meaningful Customer Experiences (New Riders; publication year 2005 or 2006 depending on listing, not verified here). The page lists them as: Accomplishment, Beauty, Creation, Community, Duty, Enlightenment, Freedom, Harmony, Justice, Oneness, Redemption, Security, Truth, Validation, Wonder. The page gives no definitions. The one-line glosses below are my own plain-language summaries, not quotes from the book.

The mapping is my interpretation of the evidence, not a finding of any study.

| Core meaning | Plain gloss (mine) | Does the evidence show it? | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accomplishment | Achieving a goal | Weak to moderate. Training wins and nosework progress; reactive-dog owners working toward calmer walks. | Duranton and Horowitz, 2019; Anthrozoös, 2024 |
| Beauty | Pleasure to the senses | Not found in this evidence. | None |
| Creation | Making something new | Not found in this evidence. | None |
| **Community** | Belonging with others | **Strong.** Dogs start conversations and neighborhood ties. | McNicholas and Collis, 2000; Wood et al., 2015 |
| **Duty** | Willingly meeting a responsibility | **Strong.** Owners control the "captive" dog's choices; walking, supervision, and care burden. | Duranton and Horowitz, 2019; Reisner et al., 2011; Mokos et al., 2025 |
| Enlightenment | Understanding clearly | Moderate. Learning to read the dog closes a measured gap. | Mariti et al., 2012; Demirbas et al., 2016 |
| Freedom | Living without unwanted limits | Moderate, **for the dog**: choice and autonomy appear to improve welfare. | Duranton and Horowitz, 2019; Horowitz, 2021 |
| Harmony | Parts fitting well together | Moderate. Play works when partners are matched; most adults play best in pairs. | Cordoni et al., 2016; VCA, 2025 |
| Justice | Fair treatment | Weak. Horowitz argues the dog deserves consideration in its own right; play is not 50:50. | Horowitz, 2021 |
| **Oneness** | Deep connection, being part of something | **Moderate to strong.** Dogs seek their owners; owners treat dogs as family. | Tuber et al., 1996; Ferrans et al., 2026; Laurent-Simpson, 2021 |
| Redemption | Recovering from failure | Weak. Fearful or reactive dogs can improve with guidance (trainer sources only). | BADRAP; AKC |
| **Security** | Freedom from worry about harm or loss | **Strong.** Safety in dog encounters, bite risk, and the owner as the dog's safe base. | Casey et al., 2013; Reisner et al., 2011; Tuber et al., 1996 |
| Truth | Honesty, knowing what is real | Moderate. Owners want to know what is normal; the evidence corrects myths (pet effect, bark translation). | Herzog, 2011; Abzaliev et al., 2024 |
| **Validation** | Being recognized as worthy | **Moderate to strong.** Pet-parent identity and disenfranchised grief are both about recognition. | Owens and Grauerholz, 2019; Spain et al., 2019 |
| Wonder | Awe at something beyond us | Moderate. What it is like to be a dog, the world of smell, dogs pressing word buttons. | Horowitz's books; Bastos et al., 2024 |

**Strongest candidates for the project:** Community, Security, Duty, Oneness, and Validation. A "small stable dog friend group" touches all five: it builds community, keeps encounters safe, helps owners do right by the dog, deepens the bond, and tells owners that a selective dog is normal.

## Top 10 findings that should shape the project

1. **The dog's essential social partner is its human; dog friends are optional and individual.** A human, not a lifelong dog companion, buffered stress (Tuber et al., 1996), and adult dogs chose owners over housemate dogs (Ferrans et al., 2026). Confidence: moderate.
2. **"My dog doesn't want dog friends" is normal.** Social maturity at 12 to 36 months often narrows tolerance (AAHA, 2015), and board-certified behaviorists say some dogs are "really happy not being social with other dogs" (Radosta). Design for selective dogs, not only social ones. Confidence: high.
3. **Small, familiar, matched groups fit the evidence better than big groups of strangers.** Most adult dogs play best in pairs (VCA, 2025), familiar pairs stay together longer (Pullen et al., 2013), lopsided play ends sooner (Cordoni et al., 2016), and dog-park time raised cortisol where an on-leash walk did not (Ottenheimer Carrier et al., 2013). No study has tested friend-matching. Playdate-matching apps exist and have mostly failed for lack of users (research/web/03-competitors-precedents.md), so the open question is the format, not the need. Confidence: moderate.
4. **Routine companionship tracks with dog health far more than money does.** Social support had about 5 times the effect of financial factors in 21,410 dogs (McCoy et al., 2023). Correlation only. Confidence: moderate.
5. **Problem behavior is the norm, and city life is linked to fear.** 72.5% of 13,715 Finnish dogs had at least one highly problematic behavior, noise fear led at 32%, and urban dogs were more fearful of dogs and strangers (Salonen et al., 2020; Puurunen et al., 2020). Owners' confusion about "what is normal" is a real, evidence-backed need. Confidence: high for prevalence, moderate for the urban link.
6. **Leashed encounters breed conflict, and city sidewalks force them** (the sidewalk part is my extrapolation). Leashed meetings showed about twice the threat displays (Řezáč et al., 2011), 22% of UK owners reported aggression toward unfamiliar dogs (Casey et al., 2013), and reactive-dog owners name off-leash dogs as a top problem (Anthrozoös, 2024). Confidence: moderate.
7. **Sniffing and choice beat toys.** Nosework improved an optimism measure (Duranton and Horowitz, 2019); toys alone did not help pet dogs (Chan et al., 2023). NYC's 6-foot leash rule makes free sniffing time scarce. Confidence: moderate.
8. **The communication gap is human literacy, not dog speech.** Owners miss quiet stress signals (Mariti et al., 2012; Demirbas et al., 2016), and 72% of children bitten in a Philadelphia study knew the dog (Reisner et al., 2011). Buttons show narrow word comprehension (Bastos et al., 2024); AI bark translation is not established (Abzaliev et al., 2024). Confidence: moderate to high.
9. **The human payoff is social and practical, not automatic happiness.** Owners walk more (Westgarth et al., 2019) and meet more neighbors (McNicholas and Collis, 2000; Wood et al., 2015), but mental-health and loneliness effects are mixed or null, and dog care can lower well-being (Herzog, 2011; Parsons et al., 2024; Mokos et al., 2025). Pitch connection and routine, not "dogs make you happy." Confidence: high that the evidence is mixed.
10. **Identity and endings matter.** Owners build "pet parent" identities (Owens and Grauerholz, 2019; Laurent-Simpson, 2021), and pet-loss grief is often disenfranchised (Spain et al., 2019). For students, 10 minutes of hands-on petting lowers cortisol (Pendry and Vandagriff, 2019). **Action item:** contact Alexandra Horowitz's Dog Cognition Lab at Barnard (ahorowit@barnard.edu) as the local expert; her 2021 paper argues for exactly the "dog as a user" stance the team is testing. Confidence: moderate.
